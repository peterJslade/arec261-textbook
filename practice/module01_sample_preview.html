Module 1 Test Bank — Sample Preview

NoteDraft preview — for review, not final

This page is a style sample: 2 questions per section × 4 sections × 3 datasets = 24 sample questions. The full bank will have 10 questions per cell (120 total). It is published here so the format and difficulty can be reviewed online before scaling up. Expand each Answer block to check the worked solution.

How this is organised. The bank is divided first by section, then by dataset within each section. Every section works across all three datasets:

  • Dataset A — Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields (1990–2025). Wide file for Sections 1–3; long file for Section 4.
  • Dataset B — Manitoba Red Spring Wheat by Variety (2020–2025). Reported = FALSE marks a yield suppressed by MASC because too few farms grew that variety there.
  • Dataset C — Canada Field Crops by Province (StatsCan, 2015–2025). Columns: Year, Province, Crop, Seeded_acres, Yield_bu_ac.

Grading model (shown to students): Visual appearance 20% + each section 20% (= 100%). Students build a separate sheet per section.

Answer values are verified against the real cleaned data (as of the current snapshot).

Section 1 — Descriptive Statistics

These questions use Dataset A — Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields

Question 1

Using the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset.

Filter to Year = 2023 and work with the Canola column.

(a) Compute the mean and the median canola yield.

(b) Compare the two values. What does the relationship between the mean and the median tell you about how yields were distributed across RMs that year, and about the low-yielding RMs in particular?

(c) Compute the standard deviation and the interquartile range (IQR) of the same column.

(d) The IQR ignores the most extreme RMs while the standard deviation does not. Given what you found in (b), explain which of these two spread measures you would trust more to describe a “typical” amount of variation across RMs, and why.

(e) A classmate uses the mean to describe “a typical RM.” Do you think the mean or the median better represents a typical RM here? Justify your choice using your answers to (a)–(d).

Answer
    1. mean ≈ 33.88, median ≈ 35.50 bu/ac.
    1. The mean falls below the median → a left/low tail: a minority of RMs with unusually low yields (down to ~3 bu/ac) pull the mean beneath the median. Most RMs cluster higher.
    1. SD ≈ 13.33 bu/ac; IQR ≈ 22.30 bu/ac (Q1 ≈ 22.60, Q3 ≈ 44.90).
    1. The IQR is more robust to the low-yield tail identified in (b); the SD is inflated by those extreme low RMs. Either can be defended if the reasoning ties back to the skew.
    1. The median is the more honest “typical” value; the mean is dragged down by the low tail. Full credit for naming the median and connecting it to the skew and spread.

Question 2

Using the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset.

Still on Year = 2023, compare the Canola column and the Barley column.

(a) Compute the mean and standard deviation of each crop’s yield.

(b) Compare the two standard deviations. If one crop’s is larger, does that by itself prove its yields were “more variable” in a meaningful sense? Explain the trap in comparing raw standard deviations across two crops with different averages.

(c) Compute the coefficient of variation (CV) for each crop.

(d) Using the CVs, state which crop’s yields were more variable relative to their average, and by how much in rough terms.

(e) Write one sentence, suitable for a farm newspaper, that correctly compares the variability of the two crops without misleading the reader.

Answer
    1. Canola: mean ≈ 33.88, SD ≈ 13.33. Barley: mean ≈ 54.56, SD ≈ 24.60.
    1. No — barley also has a much higher mean, so a bigger SD is partly just scale. Comparing raw SDs across crops with different means is the trap.
    1. CV = SD/mean. Canola ≈ 0.39; Barley ≈ 0.45.
    1. Barley is slightly more variable relative to its mean (CV 0.45 vs 0.39) — a modest difference, not a dramatic one.
    1. e.g. “Relative to their average yields, Saskatchewan barley yields in 2023 were a little more variable than canola (CV 0.45 vs 0.39).”

These questions use Dataset B — Manitoba Red Spring Wheat by Variety

Question 3

Using the Manitoba Wheat Variety dataset.

Work with the reported yields only (Reported = TRUE, i.e. non-blank Yield_bu_ac).

(a) Compute the mean and the median of the reported yields.

(b) Compare the two values, and describe in Module-1 terms what their relationship implies about the shape of the yield distribution.

(c) Compute the standard deviation, the IQR, and the coefficient of variation of the reported yields.

(d) The coefficient of variation for the Saskatchewan 2023 canola data (Question A2) was about 0.39. Compare it to the CV you found here, and interpret the contrast in one sentence a grower would understand.

(e) Based on the shape (b) and the spread measures (c), would you describe this variety-trial data as more or less consistent than the Saskatchewan field data, and why might that be the case agronomically?

Answer
    1. mean ≈ 61.16, median ≈ 62.20 bu/ac.
    1. Mean ≈ median → roughly symmetric, unlike the left-skewed SK canola.
    1. SD ≈ 12.38, IQR ≈ 15.70, CV ≈ 0.20.
    1. CV ≈ 0.20 here vs ≈ 0.39 for SK canola — e.g. “these wheat yields vary about half as much, relative to their average, as SK canola did in 2023.”
    1. More consistent (symmetric, lower CV) — controlled variety trials on established ground produce tighter, more centred yields than whole-region field averages.

Question 4

Using the Manitoba Wheat Variety dataset.

Continue with the reported yields.

(a) Compute the 90th percentile of the reported yields and state, in plain language, what it means.

(b) Compute the minimum reported yield.

(c) Look at how the minimum compares to the rest of the data. If it sits well below the others, give one genuine agronomic reason such a low wheat yield could really occur — before assuming it is a data-entry error.

(d) Explain what would happen to the mean, versus the median, if that single lowest value were removed — and why the two measures respond differently to one extreme value.

(e) Which single number — mean or median — would you headline in a report titled “typical Manitoba wheat yield,” and why?

Answer
    1. P90 ≈ 75.8 bu/ac — 90% of reported results were at or below this; only the top 10% exceeded it.
    1. minimum ≈ 4.5 bu/ac.
    1. A localized drought, hail, flood, or failed field can genuinely yield a handful of bushels — low but real.
    1. Removing it would raise the mean noticeably (the mean sums every value, so an extreme low drags it down) but barely move the median (a position, unaffected by how far away the extreme is).
    1. The median — it is not distorted by the low extreme, so it better represents “typical.”

These questions use Dataset C — Canada Field Crops by Province

Question 5

Using the Canada Field Crops dataset.

Filter to Spring wheat (all provinces, all years).

(a) Compute the mean, median, and range of spring wheat yield.

(b) The range is large. Explain what pooling many provinces (a maritime province together with the Prairies) does to a single national mean, and why that mean might describe no actual province well.

(c) Compute the standard deviation and the coefficient of variation of the pooled spring wheat yields.

(d) Suggest a better way, using only Module-1 tools, to summarise “spring wheat yield in Canada” than one overall mean and standard deviation.

(e) In one sentence, explain to a reader why “the average Canadian spring wheat yield” can be a technically correct but practically misleading statistic.

Answer
    1. mean ≈ 51.3, median ≈ 50.8, range ≈ 33.7 to 73.9 bu/ac.
    1. Pooling provinces with very different yields hides structure — the national mean sits between groups and describes none of them accurately.
    1. SD ≈ 7.5, CV ≈ 0.15 (as of snapshot).
    1. Break it out by province (AVERAGEIF per province or a PivotTable), and/or a box plot per province — summarise the groups, not the pool.
    1. e.g. “A single national average blends provinces with very different growing conditions, so it can be arithmetically right yet represent no real farm.”

Question 6

Using the Canada Field Crops dataset.

Filter to Canola (all provinces, all years).

(a) Compute the mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation of canola yield.

(b) Compare this canola CV to the spring wheat CV you found in Question C1(c), and state which crop’s provincial yields are more consistent across the country.

(c) In the raw StatsCan table (before cleaning), one province-year listed a canola yield of “0 bu/ac.” Explain why treating that 0 as a real yield would distort the mean and the standard deviation.

(d) Explain what that 0 most likely represented, and why the cleaned file treats it as missing rather than as the number zero.

(e) State the general rule this illustrates about the difference between “a measured value of zero” and “no data.”

Answer
    1. mean ≈ 41.0, SD ≈ 5.3, CV ≈ 0.13 (as of snapshot).
    1. Canola CV (≈0.13) is a little below spring wheat’s (≈0.15) → canola’s provincial yields are slightly more consistent.
    1. A false 0 pulls the mean down and inflates the SD (it looks like a huge deviation), corrupting both.
    1. It was a placeholder for negligible/near-zero acreage (e.g. Newfoundland), not a real measured yield — so it is treated as missing.
    1. A blank means “no data”; 0 means “a real measurement of zero.” Never substitute one for the other.

Section 2 — Conditional Functions & Lookups

These questions use Dataset A — Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields

Question 7

Using the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset.

Use the full (all-years) dataset.

(a) Use AVERAGEIF to find the average canola yield in each of 2010, 2021, and 2023.

(b) Rank those three years by average yield, and identify which one stands out as unusually low.

(c) Describe what feature of the whole dataset (not just canola) you could check to decide whether that low year was a broad, region-wide event rather than a canola-specific problem.

(d) Your AVERAGEIF for 2023 returns a number even though several RMs have a blank canola cell that year. Explain what AVERAGEIF did with those blanks.

(e) Explain why filling those blanks with 0 before averaging would give a wrong answer.

Answer
    1. 2010 ≈ 29.82, 2021 ≈ 21.86, 2023 ≈ 33.88 bu/ac. =AVERAGEIF(A:A, 2021, E:E).
    1. 2021 is far the lowest — the standout.
    1. Check whether other crops also fell in 2021; a fall across all crops points to a region-wide cause (drought) rather than a canola-only issue.
    1. AVERAGEIF skips blank cells, averaging only the RMs that reported.
    1. Filling blanks with 0 would count “no data” as “a yield of zero,” dragging the average down artificially.

Question 8

Using the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset.

(a) Use COUNTIF to count how many RM-year rows have a canola yield above 40 bu/ac across all years.

(b) Use a two-criteria lookup (XLOOKUP on a combined RM-and-Year key, or INDEX/MATCH) to pull the canola yield for RM 1 in 2023.

(c) Explain why a plain VLOOKUP on RM number alone would return a misleading result for this dataset.

(d) Suppose you COUNTIF for yields above 40 and separately COUNT all non-blank canola cells. Explain what dividing the first by the second would tell you, in plain language.

(e) Would that proportion be a fair way to describe “how often canola does well” if some early years have many blank cells? Explain the caution.

Answer
    1. ≈ 1483 rows. =COUNTIF(E:E, ">40").
    1. ≈ 36.8 bu/ac. =XLOOKUP(1&"|"&2023, B:B&"|"&A:A, E:E).
    1. RM 1 appears in ~36 years; VLOOKUP on RM alone returns the first match (one arbitrary year), not 2023 — you must match RM and Year.
    1. It gives the share of reported RM-years with canola above 40 bu/ac — a rough “how often yields were high.”
    1. Caution: if some years are under-reported (many blanks), the proportion is computed over an uneven set of years and can mislead — it reflects reported rows, not all RM-years.

Question 9

Using the Manitoba Wheat Variety dataset.

(a) Use COUNTIF to count the suppressed rows (Reported = FALSE) and the reported rows (TRUE).

(b) State which is larger.

(c) Explain what a suppressed row means here, in your own words.

(d) Explain why this is a different kind of “missing” than a blank cell in the Saskatchewan file.

(e) A student deletes all suppressed rows before analysing. Give one situation where that is harmless and one where it would bias a conclusion.

Answer
    1. ≈ 2960 FALSE, ≈ 2398 TRUE. =COUNTIF(G:G, "FALSE").
    1. Suppressed rows are more numerous.
    1. The variety was grown in that municipality, but too few farms reported for MASC to publish a number (privacy threshold).
    1. The SK blanks mean the crop wasn’t grown; the MB suppression means it was grown but the number is withheld.
    1. Harmless: averaging yield among reported results. Biased: claiming “variety X is rarely grown” — it may be widely grown but suppressed.

(a) Use AVERAGEIF to compute the average reported yield for the variety AAC BRANDON (BW 932).

(b) Use COUNTIF to count how many reported rows that variety has.

    1. Any valid municipality-year lookup, e.g. =XLOOKUP(...).
    1. One municipality-year reflects that place’s weather/soil that year; a variety decision should rest on the average over many sites and years.
    1. An average over 519 reports is far more trustworthy than one over a handful; the count shows how much weight each average deserves.

Question 11

Using the Canada Field Crops dataset.

(a) Use SUMIF to compute the total canola seeded acres across all provinces in 2023.

(b) Use SUMIF per province (or a sort) to identify which province contributes the most to that total.

(c) State what that dominance implies about where the “national” canola figure really comes from.

(d) Explain why summing Seeded_acres across provinces is meaningful, but summing Yield_bu_ac across provinces is not.

(e) Describe, in one sentence, the correct way to get a national yield figure from provincial yields (in words — no new formula needed).

Answer
    1. ≈ 22,085,300 acres.
    1. Saskatchewan contributes the most (largest seeded area).
    1. National canola figures are largely a Saskatchewan story.
    1. Acres are an extensive quantity and add up; yield is a rate (bu per acre), and adding rates across provinces is meaningless.
    1. Weight each province’s yield by its acres (an acreage-weighted average) rather than a plain average.

Question 13

Using the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset.

Filter to Year = 2023, Canola column.

(a) Build a histogram of 2023 canola yields (about 10–15 bins), with a descriptive title and labelled axes.

(b) Describe the shape of the distribution in Module-1 terms (symmetric, or skewed — and in which direction).

(c) Point to the specific feature of your histogram that supports the shape you described.

(d) Based on that shape, would you summarise the centre with the mean or the median? Justify it.

(e) Explain one change to the bin width that would make the shape harder to read, and why.

Answer
    1. Histogram; title e.g. “Distribution of Canola Yields Across Saskatchewan RMs, 2023”; x = “Yield (bu/ac)”, y = “Number of RMs”. (Chart visual quality is part of this section’s mark.)
    1. Left/low-skewed.
    1. The long thin tail stretching toward the low single digits, with the bulk in the 30s–40s.
    1. The median, because the low tail pulls the mean below the typical value.
    1. Too few bins hides the tail (looks symmetric); too many bins turns it into noise — either obscures the shape.

Question 14

Using the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset.

(a) Build a single box-and-whisker chart comparing Canola, Spring Wheat, and Barley yields in 2023 (three boxes), titled with labelled axes.

(b) Identify which crop has the widest box.

(c) Explain what the width of the box represents, and restate it in plain language about that crop’s yields.

(d) Explain how the chart marks outliers, and what the whiskers represent.

(e) A reader asks “which crop is most reliable to grow?” Explain what this box plot can and cannot tell them, staying within what the chart actually shows.

Answer
    1. Box-and-whisker, three crops, titled/labelled.
    1. Barley has the widest box.
    1. Box width = the interquartile range, the spread of the middle 50% of RMs; so barley yields varied most across RMs.
    1. Outliers appear as individual dots beyond the whiskers (by the 1.5×IQR rule); the whiskers reach to the most extreme non-outlier values.
    1. It shows spread and centre (a wider box = less consistent yields), which speaks to yield variability. It says nothing about price, cost, or profit — so “most reliable to grow” can only be answered on the yield-consistency part.

Question 16

Using the Manitoba Wheat Variety dataset.

(a) Identify the six most-reported varieties, and build a box plot comparing their yield distributions (one box each), titled with labelled axes.

(b) Explain what a reader learns from this box plot that a simple bar chart of average yield per variety would hide.

(c) Identify, from your chart, which of the six varieties looks the most consistent (least spread), and say how you can tell.

Answer
    1. Box plot, up to six varieties, titled/labelled.

    1. The variety with the narrowest box and shortest whiskers is the most consistent.
    1. Defensible either way if justified: a bar-of-means is cleaner for a lay audience whose takeaway is “similar averages”; the box plot is right if the message is about consistency. Credit the reasoning.
    1. e.g. a missing y-axis label or a truncated axis could exaggerate small differences between varieties.

Question 23

Using the Canada Field Crops dataset.

(a) Build a PivotTable of average canola yield by province for 2023 (Province on Rows, Yield as Average, Year filtered to 2023). Rank the provinces.

(b) Identify the top-ranked province and Saskatchewan’s rank.

(c) Explain what this pivot does and does not establish about which province is the more important canola province.

(d) Add Seeded_acres as a second value (Sum). Describe how looking at yield and acreage together changes the story.

(e) State the general lesson about distinguishing “yield per acre” from “total production.”

Answer
    1. Ranking by average yield, 2023.
    1. Ontario ranks top (~53); Saskatchewan is lower (~37).
    1. The pivot ranks yield per acre; it does not establish importance/output.
    1. Yield shows per-acre performance, acreage shows scale — SK’s far larger acreage means it produces much more canola overall despite lower yield.
    1. High yield per acre ≠ high total production; you need acreage to judge importance.

Question 24

Using the Canada Field Crops dataset.

(a) Build a PivotTable with Province on Rows and Year on Columns (canola Yield as Average).

(b) Reading across Saskatchewan’s row, identify its weakest year and describe the gap to a normal year.

(c) Reading down the 2021 column, describe what you notice across provinces.

(d) Explain how this grid lets you tell whether 2021 was a local Saskatchewan problem or a broader one.

(e) State your conclusion about 2021 and the evidence in the grid that supports it.

Answer
    1. Province × Year grid of average canola yield.
    1. Saskatchewan’s 2021 is well below its other years — a substantial drop.
    1. Multiple provinces are low in 2021, not just Saskatchewan.
    1. If the whole 2021 column is low, the cause is broad; if only SK’s cell is low, it’s local.
    1. 2021 was a widespread (multi-province) drought — several provinces dip together in that column.
