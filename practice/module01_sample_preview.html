Module 1 Test Bank — Sample Preview

NoteDraft preview — for review, not final

This page is a style sample: 2 questions per section × 4 sections × 3 datasets = 24 sample questions. The full bank will have 10 questions per cell (120 total). It is published here so the format and difficulty can be reviewed online before scaling up. Expand each Answer block to check the worked solution.

Section order: 1) Descriptive Statistics → 2) Conditional Functions & Lookups → 3) Charts → 4) PivotTables.

Grading model (shown to students): Visual appearance 20% + each section 20% (= 100%). Students build a separate sheet per section.

Numbers below are verified against the real cleaned data (as of the current snapshot).

DATASET A — Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields (1990–2025)

Wide file for Sections 1–3; long file for Section 4.

Section 1 — Descriptive Statistics

A-1-1

Filter to Year = 2023 and work with the Canola column.

(a) Compute the mean and median canola yield. (b) The mean (≈33.9) comes out below the median (≈35.5). In one or two sentences, explain what that gap tells you about how yields were distributed across RMs that year — in particular, what it implies about the low-yielding RMs. (c) A classmate reports the mean as 33.9 and concludes “so a typical RM grew about 34 bu/ac.” Do you agree that the mean is the best summary of a typical RM here? Justify your answer using the mean-vs-median comparison.

Answer
    1. mean ≈ 33.88, median ≈ 35.50 bu/ac.
    1. Mean below median signals a left/low tail — a minority of RMs with unusually low yields (down to ~3 bu/ac) drag the mean beneath the median. Most RMs cluster higher; a few poor ones pull the average down.
    1. The median (≈35.5) is the more honest “typical” value; the mean is depressed by the low-yield tail. Full credit for naming the median as more representative and tying it to the skew.

A-1-2

Still on 2023, compare Canola and Barley.

(a) Compute the standard deviation of each. Barley’s SD (≈24.6) is much larger than canola’s (≈13.3). Does that alone prove barley yields are “more variable” in a meaningful sense? Explain the trap. (b) Compute the coefficient of variation (CV) for each and use it to make a fair comparison of relative variability. (c) Write one sentence a farm reporter could publish, correctly stating which crop’s yields were more variable relative to their average.

Answer
    1. No — barley also has a much higher mean (≈54.6 vs ≈33.9), so a bigger SD is partly just scale. Comparing raw SDs across crops with different means is the trap.
    1. CV = SD/mean. Canola ≈ 13.3/33.9 ≈ 0.39; Barley ≈ 24.6/54.6 ≈ 0.45. Barley is slightly more variable relative to its mean.
    1. e.g. “Relative to their average yields, Saskatchewan barley yields in 2023 were a bit more variable than canola (CV 0.45 vs 0.39).”

Section 2 — Conditional Functions & Lookups

A-2-1

Use the full (all-years) dataset.

(a) Use AVERAGEIF to find the average canola yield in 2010, 2021, and 2023. (b) 2021 (≈21.9) sits far below 2010 (≈29.8) and 2023 (≈33.9). Which single word best describes 2021 in Saskatchewan agriculture, and what feature of the data would you point to as evidence? (c) Your AVERAGEIF for 2023 returns a number even though six RMs have a blank canola cell that year. Explain what AVERAGEIF did with those blanks, and why filling them with 0 would have been wrong.

Answer
    1. 2010 ≈ 29.82, 2021 ≈ 21.86, 2023 ≈ 33.88 bu/ac. =AVERAGEIF(A:A, 2021, E:E).
    1. Drought. Evidence: every crop’s average (not just canola) drops in 2021 — a broad, year-wide fall rather than one crop.
    1. AVERAGEIF skips blank cells, averaging only the RMs that reported. Filling blanks with 0 would count “no data” as “a yield of zero,” dragging the average down artificially.

A-2-2

(a) Use COUNTIF to count how many RM-year rows have canola yield above 40 bu/ac across all years. (b) Use a two-criteria lookup (XLOOKUP on a combined RM+Year key, or INDEX/MATCH) to pull the canola yield for RM 1 in 2023. (c) Suppose you instead used a plain VLOOKUP on RM number alone to get “RM 1’s canola yield.” Explain why that would return a misleading answer for a dataset that spans 1990–2025.

Answer
    1. ≈ 1483 rows. =COUNTIF(E:E, ">40").
    1. ≈ 36.8 bu/ac. =XLOOKUP(1&"|"&2023, B:B&"|"&A:A, E:E).
    1. RM 1 appears in ~36 different years; VLOOKUP on RM alone returns the first match only (one arbitrary year), not the 2023 value. You must match on RM and Year.

Section 3 — Charts

A-3-1

Filter to Year = 2023, Canola column.

(a) Build a histogram of 2023 canola yields (≈10–15 bins), with a descriptive title and labelled axes. (b) Describe the shape in Module-1 terms (symmetric? skewed? which direction?), and point to the specific feature of the histogram that supports your description. (c) Would you summarize the centre of this histogram with the mean or the median? Tie your choice to the shape you described in (b).

Answer
    1. Histogram; title e.g. “Distribution of Canola Yields Across Saskatchewan RMs, 2023”, x = “Yield (bu/ac)”, y = “Number of RMs”.
    1. Left/low-skewed — the bulk sits in the 30s–40s with a longer thin tail stretching toward the low single digits.
    1. The median, because the low tail pulls the mean below the typical value. (Grading: 20% of this section’s marks are the chart’s visual quality — title, axis labels, sensible bins.)

A-3-2

(a) Build a single box-and-whisker chart comparing Canola, Spring Wheat, and Barley yields in 2023 (three boxes). (b) Which crop shows the widest box, and what does box width represent? State what that means about that crop’s yields in plain language. (c) A reader asks “which crop is most reliable to grow?” Explain what the box plot can and cannot tell them about that — staying within what this chart actually shows.

Answer
    1. Box-and-whisker, three crops; titled, axes labelled.
    1. Barley has the widest box (IQR ≈ 41 vs canola ≈ 22). Box width = the interquartile range, the spread of the middle 50% of RMs — so barley yields varied most across RMs.
    1. It shows spread and centre (a wider/lower box = less consistent yields), which speaks to variability. It does not show profitability, price, or cost, so “most reliable to grow” can only be answered on the yield-consistency part. (No probability/likelihood language — not yet introduced.)

Section 4 — PivotTables

Use the long file (rm_yields_1990plus_long.csv).

A-4-1

(a) Build a PivotTable of average yield by crop for 2023 (Year in Filters = 2023, Crop on Rows, Yield as Average). Rank the crops. (b) Lentils appear at ≈1314 while the next crop is ≈71. Explain why — and what a reader would wrongly conclude if they took the ranking at face value. (c) Add Unit to Filters and set it to bu/ac. Now which crop is highest and which lowest, and why is this the fair comparison?

Answer
    1. Lentils ≈1314 (lb/ac), Oats ≈71, Barley ≈55, Spring Wheat ≈43, Peas ≈34, Canola ≈34, Durum ≈30, Flax ≈20 (bu/ac).
    1. Lentils are in lb/ac, everything else bu/ac. Taken at face value a reader would think lentils out-yield oats ~18×, which is a unit artifact, not agronomy.
    1. Filtered to bu/ac: Oats highest (≈71), Flax lowest (≈20). Fair because all crops share one unit.

A-4-2

(a) Build a PivotTable with Year on Rows and Crop on Columns (Yield as Average, Unit filtered to bu/ac). Read down the columns. (b) Compare canola in 2021 (≈21.9) vs 2023 (≈33.9). Describe the size of the gap in plain language. (c) 2021 is low for every crop, not just canola. Explain how the crop-by-year grid lets you distinguish a whole-year shock (like drought) from a problem affecting only one crop.

Answer
    1. Year × Crop grid of average yields.
    1. 2021 canola is about one-third lower than 2023 (≈12 bu/ac less) — a large drop.
    1. If a whole row (year) is low across all crop columns, the cause is broad (weather/drought). If a single column (crop) dips in one year while others hold, the cause is crop-specific (a pest/disease). The grid shows rows vs columns at a glance.

DATASET B — Manitoba Red Spring Wheat by Variety (2020–2025)

Single file mb_wheat_varieties.csv; Reported = TRUE/FALSE (FALSE = MASC suppressed the yield because too few farms grew it).

Section 1 — Descriptive Statistics

B-1-1

Work with the reported yields only (Reported = TRUE, i.e. non-blank Yield_bu_ac).

(a) Compute the mean, median, and IQR of reported yields. (b) Mean ≈61.2 and median ≈62.2 are very close. What does that near-equality suggest about the shape, compared with the skewed Saskatchewan canola data? (c) The CV here is ≈0.20, versus ≈0.39 for SK 2023 canola. Interpret that contrast in one sentence a grower would understand.

Answer
    1. mean ≈61.16, median ≈62.20, IQR ≈15.7 bu/ac.
    1. Mean ≈ median → roughly symmetric, unlike the left-skewed SK canola. Variety trials on established ground give tighter, more centred yields.
    1. e.g. “Relative to their average, these wheat yields vary about half as much as Saskatchewan canola did in 2023 (CV 0.20 vs 0.39) — a more consistent crop-and-place.”

B-1-2

(a) Compute the 90th percentile of reported yields and state, in plain language, what it means. (b) Compute the minimum reported yield. It is far below the rest. Before calling it an error, give one real agronomic reason a genuine 4–5 bu/ac wheat yield could occur. (c) Explain why the median barely moves if that one low value is removed, but the mean would shift more — using what you know about how each responds to extreme values.

Answer
    1. P90 ≈ 75.8 bu/ac — 90% of reported variety-municipality results were at or below this; only the top 10% exceeded it.
    1. A localized drought/hail/flood or a failed field can genuinely yield a handful of bushels — low, but real, not necessarily a typo.
    1. The median is a position (the middle value) and ignores how far away extremes are, so one low point barely moves it; the mean sums every value, so an extreme low pulls it down. This is the module’s mean-vs-outlier point.

Section 2 — Conditional Functions & Lookups

B-2-1

(a) Use COUNTIF/COUNTIFS to count how many rows are suppressed (Reported = FALSE) vs reported (TRUE). (b) Suppressed rows outnumber reported ones (≈2960 vs ≈2398). Explain what a suppressed row means here, and why it is a different kind of “missing” than a blank in the Saskatchewan file. (c) A student deletes all suppressed rows before analysing. Give one situation where that is fine and one where it would bias a conclusion.

Answer
    1. ≈2960 FALSE, ≈2398 TRUE. =COUNTIF(G:G, "FALSE").
    1. Suppressed = the variety was grown in that municipality but too few farms reported for MASC to publish a number (privacy). Different from the SK blanks, which mean the crop wasn’t grown at all.
    1. Fine: computing the average yield among reported results. Biased: claiming “variety X is rarely grown” from few reported rows — it may be widely grown but suppressed. Absence of a number ≠ absence of the crop.

B-2-2

(a) Use AVERAGEIF to compute the average reported yield for the variety AAC BRANDON (BW 932). (b) Use a lookup to find AAC Brandon’s yield in a specific municipality-year of your choice, then say why a single such number is a weak basis for choosing a variety. (c) Explain why comparing two varieties fairly requires accounting for how many municipalities reported each (hint: COUNTIF on the variety), not just their average yields.

Answer
    1. ≈ 59.6 bu/ac (most-grown variety, n≈519 reported).
    1. A single municipality-year reflects that place’s weather/soil that year; variety choice should rest on the average across many sites/years, not one point.
    1. An average over 5 reports is far less trustworthy than one over 500; a variety can look “best” off a handful of lucky sites. Report counts show how much weight each average deserves.

Section 3 — Charts

B-3-1

(a) Build a histogram of all reported wheat yields, titled with labelled axes. (b) Describe the shape and contrast it with the Saskatchewan canola histogram from Dataset A. (c) By the 1.5×IQR rule, this data has genuine low-end outliers (≈39 of them). Explain how a box plot would display them and why a histogram alone might not make you call them “outliers”.

Answer
    1. Histogram, titled/labelled.
    1. Roughly symmetric and tighter (bulk 54–70) vs the left-skewed, wider SK canola — reflecting controlled variety trials.
    1. A box plot marks points beyond Q1−1.5·IQR (here below ≈30) as individual dots past the whisker, flagging them explicitly; a histogram just shows a low tail, leaving “outlier” a judgment call. This is the module’s box-plot outlier convention.

B-3-2

(a) Pick the six most-reported varieties and build a box plot comparing their yield distributions (one box each). (b) Explain what a reader learns from the box plot that a simple bar chart of average yield per variety would hide. (c) You want to show a non-technical audience “these top varieties yield similarly.” Would you choose the box plot or a bar chart of means, and why? (Answer in terms of audience and message, per the module’s chart-choice guidance.)

Answer
    1. Box plot, up to six varieties, titled/labelled.
    1. The box plot shows spread and consistency (some varieties more variable than others) and flags outliers — a bar of means shows only the centre and hides all of that.
    1. Defensible either way if justified: bar-of-means is cleaner for a lay audience whose takeaway is “similar averages”; the box plot is right if the point is consistency. Credit the reasoning about audience + message, not the specific pick.

Section 4 — PivotTables

B-4-1

(a) Build a PivotTable of average reported yield by variety (Variety on Rows, Yield_bu_ac as Average). Add a Count to see how many reports back each average. (b) The top variety by average (SY MANNESS, ≈72) beats the most-grown variety (AAC Brandon, ≈60). Explain why “highest average” and “most widely grown” can differ, and which you’d trust more for a planting decision. (c) Explain why filtering the PivotTable to varieties with a minimum number of reports (say ≥30) gives a more trustworthy ranking than including every variety.

Answer
    1. Variety on Rows; Yield as Average + Count.
    1. A niche variety on a few good sites can post a high average without being widely adopted; AAC Brandon’s ≈60 rests on ~519 reports. Trust the widely-reported average as a decision base; a high average on thin data is fragile.
    1. Averages from 1–2 reports are noisy and can top the list by luck; a ≥30 threshold keeps only well-supported estimates, making the ranking meaningful.

B-4-2

(a) Build a PivotTable with Variety on Rows and Year on Columns (Yield as Average) for AAC Brandon and a few common varieties. (b) AAC Brandon dips in 2021 (≈49) versus ≈62–66 in other years. Given what you saw in the Saskatchewan data, what most likely caused the 2021 dip across Manitoba varieties too? (c) Explain how looking at the same variety across years separates a variety’s genetic potential from year-to-year weather effects.

Answer
    1. Variety × Year grid.
    1. The 2021 drought hit Manitoba as well — the dip appears across varieties in that year, i.e. a weather effect, not a variety fault. (Nice cross-dataset consistency with the SK 2021 drop.)
    1. Holding the variety fixed and reading across years isolates weather (all years share the same genetics), while comparing varieties within a year isolates genetics (all share the same weather).

DATASET C — Canada Field Crops by Province (StatsCan, 2015–2025)

Single file statcan_field_crops.csv: columns Year, Province, Crop, Seeded_acres, Yield_bu_ac.

Section 1 — Descriptive Statistics

C-1-1

Filter to Spring wheat (all provinces, all years).

(a) Compute mean, median, and range of spring wheat yield. (b) The range is large because provinces differ hugely (a maritime province vs the Prairies). Explain why a single national mean can be a misleading summary when the data pools very different provinces. (c) Suggest a better way to summarize “spring wheat yield in Canada” than one overall mean, using only Module-1 tools.

Answer
    1. mean ≈ 51.3, median ≈ 50.8, range ≈ 33.7 to 73.9 bu/ac.
    1. Pooling provinces with very different yields hides real structure — the “national average” describes no actual province well.
    1. Break it out by province (a PivotTable or AVERAGEIF per province), and/or show a box plot per province — summarize the groups, not just the pool.

C-1-2

Filter to Canola (all provinces, all years).

(a) Compute the CV of canola yield and compare it to the CV of spring wheat from C-1-1. (b) Interpret which crop’s provincial yields are more consistent across the country, in one plain sentence. (c) A colleague computed a canola yield of “0 bu/ac” for one province-year in the raw StatsCan table. Explain why treating that 0 as a real yield would distort the mean, and what it more likely represented.

Answer
    1. Canola CV ≈ 0.13 vs spring wheat CV ≈ 0.15 (both computed SD/mean).
    1. e.g. “Across provinces and years, canola yields were slightly more consistent than spring wheat, relative to their averages.”
    1. A real 0 would pull the mean down and isn’t meaningful (no crop truly yields 0 on 0 acres); it was a placeholder/negligible-acreage code, correctly treated as missing (why the cleaned file drops it).

Section 2 — Conditional Functions & Lookups

C-2-1

(a) Use SUMIF to compute the total canola seeded acres across all provinces in 2023. (b) The total is ≈22.1 million acres. Using SUMIF per province (or a quick sort), name which province dominates that total and what that implies about where the “national” canola number really comes from. (c) Explain why summing Seeded_acres is valid but summing Yield_bu_ac across provinces would be meaningless.

Answer
    1. ≈ 22,085,300 acres. =SUMIF on Crop=Canola, Year=2023.
    1. Saskatchewan dominates (largest seeded area), so national canola figures are largely a Saskatchewan story.
    1. Acres are an extensive quantity — they add up. Yield (bu per acre) is a rate/intensity; adding rates across provinces has no meaning (you’d average them, ideally weighted by acres).

C-2-2

(a) Use COUNTIF/COUNTIFS to count how many province-crop-year rows have a blank yield. (b) Blanks concentrate in certain crop-province pairs (e.g. flax or durum in eastern provinces). Explain what a blank means here and why it is expected, not an error. (c) A lookup for “durum yield in Nova Scotia, 2022” returns blank. Explain, in one sentence, the correct way to report that result rather than writing 0 or “error”.

Answer
    1. ≈ 185 rows blank (as of snapshot). =COUNTBLANK or COUNTIF on the yield column.
    1. Blank = that crop isn’t meaningfully grown in that province/year, so StatsCan publishes no yield — expected geography, not a data fault.
    1. Report it as “not grown / not reported” (leave blank), never 0 — 0 would falsely imply a real measured zero yield.

Section 3 — Charts

C-3-1

(a) Build a box plot comparing canola yield across provinces (one box per province) using all years 2015–2025. (b) Which provinces sit highest, and which show the most spread? Say what “spread” means for a province here. (c) A headline claims “Ontario grows the best canola.” Explain what your box plot supports and what it does not (e.g., scale of production), staying within the chart.

Answer
    1. Box plot, province on the category axis.
    1. Ontario highest average (~47), Prairie provinces mid; spread = the range/IQR of a province’s year-to-year yields — wider box = more variable across years.
    1. The box plot supports “higher yield per acre in Ontario,” but says nothing about how much canola each province grows (acreage) — Saskatchewan produces vastly more despite lower per-acre yield. Yield ≠ production.

C-3-2

(a) Using a PivotTable feeding a chart, make a line chart of average Canadian canola yield by year (2015–2025). (b) Identify the lowest year on the chart and connect it to what you have seen in the other two datasets. (c) Would a line chart or a pie chart be the right tool to show this “yield over time” story? Justify using the module’s chart-choice guidance.

Answer
    1. Line chart, year on x, average yield on y, titled/labelled.
    1. 2021 is the low point — the same drought that hit the SK RM data and Manitoba varieties. Cross-dataset consistency.
    1. Line chart — it shows change over an ordered variable (time). A pie chart shows parts of a whole at one moment and cannot show a trend; the module explicitly cautions against pie charts here.

Section 4 — PivotTables

C-4-1

(a) Build a PivotTable of average canola yield by province for 2023 (Province on Rows, Yield as Average, Year filtered to 2023). (b) Rank the provinces. Ontario (~53) tops Saskatchewan (~37). Does that mean Ontario is the more important canola province? Explain what this pivot does and does not establish. (c) Add Seeded_acres as a second value (Sum). Explain how looking at yield and acreage together changes the story from yield alone.

Answer
    1. Province on Rows, Yield Average, Year=2023.
    1. Ranking is by yield per acre, on which Ontario leads. It does not establish importance/output — Saskatchewan’s far larger acreage means it produces much more canola overall.
    1. Yield shows per-acre performance; acreage shows scale. Together: Ontario yields more per acre but SK dominates total production. The two columns tell a fuller story than either alone.

C-4-2

(a) Build a PivotTable with Province on Rows and Year on Columns (canola Yield as Average). (b) Reading across Saskatchewan’s row, identify its weakest year and state the gap to a normal year in plain language. (c) Explain how this province × year grid lets you tell whether 2021 was a local Saskatchewan problem or a national one — and what you conclude.

Answer
    1. Province × Year grid of average canola yield.
    1. Saskatchewan’s 2021 is well below its other years — a substantial drop (SK canola ~22 vs ~37 in 2023 in the RM data; the provincial StatsCan figure shows the same direction).
    1. If 2021 is low down the whole column (most provinces), it’s a broad/national drought year; if only SK’s cell is low, it’s local. Reading the column shows multiple Prairie provinces down together → widespread drought.

Notes for review

  • Every question has a compute part AND an interpret/communicate part — the interpretation is the point, calculation is the setup.
  • Vocabulary stays inside Module 1: symmetric/skewed, mean-vs-median, range/IQR/SD/CV, percentiles, box-plot whiskers & 1.5×IQR outliers, histogram shape, pie-vs-bar/line chart choice, wide/long, pivot grouping, yield-vs-production, rate-vs-total. No normality, correlation, probability, sampling, significance.
  • Cross-dataset thread: the 2021 drought recurs in all three, so students who do multiple quizzes see the same real event three ways.
  • Chart & pivot sections lean hardest on presentation and reading, matching the 20%-visual weighting.
  • Section 4 answers assume the SK long file; Datasets B and C are already long-shaped.
