Module 1 — Full Test Bank

NoteHow this test bank works

This bank holds 20 questions: five in each of four sections. On the real test you receive four questions — one from each section — and you submit an .xlsx workbook with a separate sheet for each section’s answers. Each question is designed to take about 8–10 minutes.

Expand each Answer block to check your work. Every value has been computed from the real data; when the source files are refreshed the exact numbers may shift slightly, but the method stays the same.

The dataset.

  • Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields (1990–2025). Average yield (bu/ac) for eight crops in every Rural Municipality. Wide file rm_yields_1990plus.csv for Sections 2–3; long file rm_yields_1990plus_long.csv for Section 4. Blank = crop not grown/reported that RM-year.

Working with a single year. Several questions ask you to work with one year of the data. Do not just apply a filter and point a formula at the whole column: as Module 1 explains, a filter hides rows from you, not from your formulas. Copy the rows you want onto a new sheet first, or use a conditional function that tests the year. If you copy 2023 out, you will have 295 rows of data.

Grading model. 10% for presentation — labelled cells, sensible formats, a reader can follow the sheet. The remaining 90% is split evenly across the four questions, and evenly across the parts within each question. Parts are marked on follow-through: if an early part is wrong but the later parts are correctly worked from your own earlier answer, the later parts still earn full marks.

Vocabulary note. Everything here uses only Module 1 tools: SUM, AVERAGE, COUNT, COUNTA, MEDIAN, MODE.SNGL, MIN, MAX, ROUND, SUMPRODUCT, STDEV.S, VAR.S, QUARTILE.INC, PERCENTILE.INC, IF, COUNTIF, SUMIF, AVERAGEIF, XLOOKUP; relative and absolute references; number and date formatting; sorting and filtering; wide vs long shape; and PivotTables.

Section 1 — Building a Worksheet

These questions are about spreadsheet mechanics: anchoring references, formatting numbers and dates, and order of operations. The data is given in the question — copy the block and paste it into Excel.

Question 1

Paste this block into a blank sheet.

Field   Acres   Yield (bu/ac)
North   120 41.2
South   240 38.6
Creek   95  44.8
Home    310 36.1
Rented  175 39.9

Off to the right of the table, in a column with nothing else in it, put the canola price $14.20 in a single labelled cell.

(a) Add a Revenue ($/ac) column: each field’s yield times the price cell. Write the formula once and fill it down. Format as currency with two decimals.

(b) Add a Total revenue ($) column: revenue per acre times that field’s acres. Format with a thousands separator and no decimals.

(c) Under the table, use SUM to total the acres, the total bushels (yield × acres), and the total revenue.

(d) In a spare column, repeat the (a) formula without the $ signs and fill it down all five rows. Label the block clearly as the incorrect version.

(e) Beside it, write one sentence saying what appears in those cells and why.

Answer
    1. With the price in F1: =C2*$F$1 → $585.04, $548.12, $636.16, $512.62, $566.58.
    1. =D2*B2 → $70,204.80 · $131,548.80 · $60,435.20 · $158,912.20 · $99,151.50.
    1. Acres 940; bushels 36,637.5; total revenue $520,252.50.
    1. The unanchored version, filled down five rows.
    1. The first row is right; every row below it returns $0.00, because the price reference walks down to F2, F3… which are empty. Excel treats an empty cell as zero here and reports no error, so the column simply fills with zeros.

Question 2

Paste this block into a blank sheet.

Field   Canola  Wheat   Barley
North   41.2    52.4    63.1
South   38.6    49.8    58.7
Creek   44.8    55.2    66.4
Home    36.1    47.3    61.9
Rented  39.9    51.0    60.2

Directly above the three yield columns, enter the prices in a single row: Canola $14.20, Wheat $8.35, Barley $5.60.

(a) To the right, build a revenue-per-acre block of the same shape. Write one formula in its top-left cell and fill it across and down to complete all fifteen cells.

(b) State the formula you used, and say which part of the price reference is locked and which is not.

(c) Under your revenue block, use AVERAGE to find the mean revenue per acre for each crop.

(d) Which crop gives the highest average revenue per acre, and by how much over the second-placed crop?

(e) A student anchors the price fully as $B$1 instead. In one sentence, say what their wheat and barley columns would show and why nothing on the sheet would look broken.

Answer
    1. With prices in row 1 and the first yield in B3: =B3*B$1, filled across and down. Any layout works provided the price row is locked and the column is not.
    1. Only the row is locked (B$1). Filling down must keep pointing at the price row; filling across must let the column move so each crop meets its own price.
    1. Canola $569.70, Wheat $427.02, Barley $347.54.
    1. Canola, by about $142.69 over wheat (569.704 − 427.019 = 142.685; accept $142.68 or $142.69 — the difference lands exactly on a half-cent).
    1. Every column would use the canola price, so wheat and barley revenues would be roughly 70% and 150% too high. The sheet still fills with plausible dollar figures, so nothing signals the error.

Question 3

Paste this block into a blank sheet.

Date    Crop    Tonnes
14 September 2026   Canola  42.5
18 September 2026   Canola  38.0
23 September 2026   Wheat   61.2
2 October 2026  Wheat   55.8
9 October 2026  Barley  47.3

(a) Make sure Excel has read the dates as dates, not text, and format them as DD-MMM-YYYY (e.g. 14-Sep-2026). Show tonnes to one decimal.

(b) Add a Days since previous column. Leave the first row blank. Make sure the results show as plain numbers.

(c) In one cell, compute the number of days from the first delivery to the last.

(d) Use SUM to total the tonnes, and AVERAGE to find the mean load size.

(e) In one sentence, explain why part (b) would fail if Excel had stored the dates as text rather than as dates.

Answer
    1. Dates formatted DD-MMM-YYYY; tonnes 0.0.
    1. =A3-A2 filled down → 4, 5, 9, 7 days. If the cell shows 04-Jan-1900 it inherited the date format; change it to Number.
    1. =A6-A2 = 25 days.
    1. Total 244.8 tonnes; mean 48.96 tonnes.
    1. Excel stores a date as a number, which is why subtracting two of them gives a count of days. Text cannot be subtracted, so the formula would return an error instead of a gap.

Question 4

Paste this block into a blank sheet.

Load    Protein
A   0.1342
B   0.1285
C   0.1401
D   0.1198
E   0.1356

(a) Copy the protein column into a second column. Format the first as General and the second as Percentage with 2 decimals. Label both with the format used.

(b) Under each column, compute the average with AVERAGE, formatting each the same way as the column above it.

(c) The two averages display differently but hold the same number. In a spare cell, write that value to four decimal places.

(d) The contract pays a premium on any load above 13.5% protein. Add a column using IF that returns Yes or No, and use COUNTIF to count how many qualify.

(e) A student writes =IF(B2>13.5,"Yes","No") and gets No for every load. In one sentence, explain the error.

Answer
    1. The same five values twice; only the format differs. General shows 0.1342, 0.1285…; Percentage shows 13.42%, 12.85%….
    1. Both averages hold the same number. General shows the full 0.13164; the percentage column shows 13.16%.
    1. 0.1316.
    1. =IF(B2>0.135,"Yes","No"); =COUNTIF(D2:D6,"Yes") = 2 (loads C and E).
    1. The cells hold proportions, not percentages — 0.1342, not 13.42. Nothing is above 13.5, so every row returns No. Formatting a cell as a percentage does not change the stored value.

Question 5

Paste this block into a blank sheet.

Field   Acres   Yield (bu/ac)
North   120 41.2
South   240 38.6
Creek   95  44.8
Home    310 36.1
Rented  175 39.9

(a) Use AVERAGE to find the plain mean of the five yields.

(b) Add a Bushels column (yield × acres) and total it, along with the acres, using SUM.

(c) The farm’s actual average yield is total bushels divided by total acres. Compute it. Format both averages to one decimal place.

(d) Compute the same figure a second way, in a single cell, using SUMPRODUCT over SUM.

(e) The two averages in (a) and (c) differ. In one or two sentences, say which one answers “what did this farm average across all its acres”, and what the plain mean is treating as equally important.

Answer
    1. =AVERAGE(C2:C6) = 40.1 bu/ac.
    1. =B2*C2 filled down; acres 940, bushels 36,637.5.
    1. =D7/B7 = 39.0 bu/ac.
    1. =SUMPRODUCT(C2:C6,B2:B6)/SUM(B2:B6) = 39.0 — the same number in one step.
    1. The weighted average in (c) answers it, because it counts every bushel once regardless of which field grew it. The plain mean treats each field as equally important, so the 95-acre Creek field — the highest yielding of the five — counts as much as the 310-acre Home field, and pulls the answer up by about 1.1 bu/ac.

Section 2 — Descriptive Statistics and Distributions

These questions use the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset (rm_yields_1990plus.csv): average yield in bushels per acre for each crop in every Rural Municipality. A blank cell means that crop was not grown or not reported in that RM that year.

Question 6

Work with the Canola column for 2023 (copy the 2023 rows to a sheet of their own first — see the note above).

(a) Compute the mean and the median canola yield.

(b) The two differ. State which is larger and, in one or two sentences, say what that tells you about how the low-yielding RMs are distributed.

(c) Compute the standard deviation and the coefficient of variation.

(d) Repeat (a) and (c) for the Barley column in the same year.

(e) Barley’s standard deviation is much larger than canola’s. Using your CVs, explain in two sentences whether barley yields were genuinely more variable, and why comparing the raw standard deviations alone would mislead.

Answer
    1. Mean 33.88, median 35.50 bu/ac (n = 289).
    1. The mean is below the median, so a minority of RMs with unusually low yields — down to 3.4 bu/ac — pull the average beneath the middle value. Most RMs sit higher than the mean suggests.
    1. SD 13.33; CV = 13.33 / 33.88 = 0.394.
    1. Barley: mean 54.56, SD 24.60, CV 0.451.
    1. Barley’s SD is nearly twice canola’s, but barley also yields far more per acre, so part of that spread is just scale. The CVs (0.451 vs 0.394) show barley was only modestly more variable relative to its own average. Comparing raw SDs across crops with different means overstates the difference.

Question 7

Work with the Spring Wheat column for 2023, using the 2023 rows you extracted.

(a) Compute the first and third quartiles with QUARTILE.INC, and the interquartile range.

(b) Compute the 10th and 90th percentiles with PERCENTILE.INC.

(c) In one sentence, say what the 10th percentile means for a farmer in this dataset.

(d) Compute the range (MAX − MIN).

(e) The range is far larger than the IQR. Explain in two sentences why, and say which of the two you would quote in a report describing a typical spread across RMs.

Answer
    1. Q1 28.50, Q3 55.60, IQR 27.10 bu/ac.
    1. 10th percentile 19.34, 90th percentile 64.92 bu/ac.
    1. One RM in ten had an average spring wheat yield at or below 19.34 bu/ac that year.
    1. 74.7 − 5.0 = 69.70 bu/ac.
    1. The range is set entirely by the two most extreme RMs, so a single failed or exceptional RM defines it. The IQR covers the middle half and ignores both tails, so it is the better description of a typical spread — quote the IQR, and mention the extremes separately if they matter.

Question 8

Work with the Canola column for 2023, using the 2023 rows you extracted.

(a) Build a histogram of the canola yields with about 10–15 bins. Give it a descriptive title and label both axes.

(b) State the mean and the median beneath the chart, and describe the shape in one or two sentences: symmetric, or skewed — and if skewed, in which direction. Say how the mean’s position relative to the median matches what you see.

(c) A classmate applies a filter to show only 2023, leaves the full file in place, and writes =AVERAGE over the whole canola column. They get 28.29 rather than your figure. In two sentences, explain what they actually computed and why the filter did not help.

(d) A classmate makes the same histogram with 3 bins and concludes the data is symmetric. In one or two sentences, explain what went wrong.

Answer
    1. Histogram, 10–15 bins, titled and labelled.
    1. Mean 33.88, median 35.50. Left-skewed — a tail toward the low yields: most RMs cluster between roughly 25 and 50 bu/ac with a thin tail down to 3.4. The mean sits below the median, which is exactly what a low tail does: the extreme values drag the average down while the middle value barely moves.
    1. They computed the mean canola yield across every year from 1990 to 2025, not 2023. A filter hides rows from the person looking at the sheet; it does nothing to the range a formula reads. The fix is to copy the 2023 rows to their own sheet, or to use a conditional function that tests the year.
    1. Three bins are too coarse to show the tail. Each bin spans about 19 bu/ac, so the low RMs get absorbed into a wide left-hand bar and the asymmetry disappears. Bin width has to be fine enough to reveal the shape without showing random noise.

Question 9

Work with the 2023 rows you extracted. You will use the Canola and Barley columns.

(a) Compute the mean of each, along with the mean of the Oats column.

(b) Use COUNT on each of the three columns. The three counts differ. In one sentence, say what that difference tells you about the dataset.

(c) Explain in one sentence why COUNT and COUNTA would give different answers if the blank cells contained the text "n/a" instead of being empty.

(d) A student fills every blank canola cell with 0 and recomputes the mean. Compute what that mean would be, and state the difference from your answer in (a).

(e) In two sentences, explain why filling blanks with zero is wrong here, and what the blank actually represents.

Answer
    1. Canola 33.88, Barley 54.56, Oats 71.14.
    1. Canola 289, Barley 285, Oats 200. Not every crop is grown or reported in every RM, so the columns have different numbers of observations.
    1. COUNT counts numbers only, so it would still return 289; COUNTA counts any non-empty cell, so it would also count the "n/a" entries and return 295 — the full number of 2023 rows.
    1. With 295 rows and six zeros, the mean falls to 33.19 — about 0.69 bu/ac lower.
    1. A blank means the crop was not grown or not reported, not that the RM harvested nothing. Entering 0 turns a missing observation into a measured failure and drags the average down.

Question 10

Using the whole dataset (all years), work with the Canola column.

(a) Compute the mean canola yield for Year = 2021 and for Year = 2022.

(b) Compute the percentage change from 2021 to 2022 and format it as a percentage with one decimal.

(c) State the formula you used and say which year belongs in the denominator, and why.

(d) Compute the standard deviation for each of the two years.

(e) In two sentences, use both the means and the standard deviations to describe what happened between the two years. Say what a single mean on its own would have hidden.

Answer
    1. 2021 mean 21.86, 2022 mean 35.40 bu/ac.
    1. =(B2-B1)/B1 = +61.9%.
    1. The starting year (2021) goes in the denominator: percentage change measures movement relative to where you began.
    1. 2021 SD 8.14; 2022 SD 12.38.
    1. Average yields rose by about three-fifths after the 2021 drought, and the spread widened as well — RMs did not all recover equally. A mean alone would have shown the recovery but hidden the fact that the gap between good and poor RMs grew.

Section 3 — Conditional Functions & Lookups

These questions use the Saskatchewan RM Crop Yields dataset (rm_yields_1990plus.csv).

Question 11

Work with the 2023 rows you extracted.

(a) Use COUNTIF to count the RMs with a canola yield above 40 bu/ac.

(b) Use COUNTIF to count those at or below 20 bu/ac.

(c) Use AVERAGEIF to find the mean barley yield in those RMs where canola came in above 40.

(d) Compute the mean barley yield across all RMs in 2023, and compare it with your answer to (c).

(e) In two sentences, say what the comparison suggests, and give one reason other than crop choice that might explain it.

Answer
    1. =COUNTIF(canola_range,">40") = 114 RMs. (In the shipped file canola is column E; use whatever range your 2023 rows occupy.)
    1. =COUNTIF(canola_range,"<=20") = 57 RMs.
    1. =AVERAGEIF(canola_range,">40",barley_range) = 77.40 bu/ac. Canola is tested, barley is averaged. Note this averages 112 RMs, not the 114 from (a): two of them have no barley figure, and AVERAGEIF skips blanks in the range it is averaging. Dividing a SUMIF by 114 instead gives 76.04 and is wrong.
    1. All-RM barley mean 54.56; the good-canola RMs averaged 22.83 bu/ac more (accept 22.84 if computed from the rounded figures).
    1. RMs that grew canola well also grew barley well, which points to something affecting both crops rather than to the crops themselves. Growing-season rainfall is the obvious candidate — soil zone and seeding date would also do it.

Question 12

Work with the 2023 rows you extracted.

(a) Use IF to add a column labelling each RM Above or Below according to whether its canola yield beats the 2023 canola mean. Compute the mean in its own cell and point the IF at that cell.

(b) Use COUNTIF to count how many RMs fall in each category.

(c) The two counts are not equal. In one or two sentences, explain what that tells you about the distribution.

(d) Now repeat (a) and (b) using the median instead of the mean.

(e) The median split is much closer to even. In two sentences, explain why that is guaranteed by the definition of the median, and what the mean split told you that the median split did not.

Answer
    1. =IF(E2>$M$1,"Above","Below") where M1 (any spare cell) holds =AVERAGE(canola_range) = 33.88.
    1. Above 153, Below 136.
    1. More RMs sit above the mean than below it (153 to 136), which is the signature of a low tail: a minority of very poor RMs drags the mean down past the middle of the data.
    1. Median 35.50 → Above 143, Below 146.
    1. The median is defined as the value with half the observations on each side, so the split is even by construction (143/146, the difference being ties at the median). The mean split is informative precisely because it is uneven — the 153/136 gap is a measure of the skew that the median split cannot show.

Question 13

Work with the 2023 rows you extracted. Build a small price table well clear of the data:

Crop    Price ($/bu)
Canola  14.20
Spring Wheat    8.35
Barley  5.60
Oats    4.50

(a) Use XLOOKUP to pull the canola price into a cell, looking it up by name rather than typing the number.

(b) Add a Canola revenue ($/ac) column: each RM’s canola yield times the looked-up price. Write it once and fill it down.

(c) Explain in one sentence which references in your formula had to be absolute.

(d) Use AVERAGE to find the mean canola revenue per acre across RMs, and MAX to find the highest.

(e) Copy your revenue column and paste it as values beside itself, labelled “at $14.20”. Now change the price in your price table to $11.50 and paste the new column as values beside that, labelled “at $11.50”. In one sentence, explain why keeping the price in a looked-up cell is better practice than typing 14.20 into the formula.

Answer
    1. With the price table pasted well clear of the data — say in L2:M5 — =XLOOKUP("Canola",$L$2:$L$5,$M$2:$M$5) = 14.20.
    1. =E2*$N$1 where N1 holds the looked-up price and canola is column E.
    1. The lookup ranges ($L$2:$L$5, $M$2:$M$5) and the price cell ($N$1) — all must stay put as the formula fills down.
    1. Mean $481.11/ac; maximum $853.42/ac.
    1. Both columns pasted as values, so the grader can see the before and after. Every revenue updated at once when the price changed: the price lives in one place, so it can be checked, sourced and changed without editing 289 formulas, and no stale number hides inside a formula.

Question 14

Work with the 2023 rows you extracted.

(a) Use SUMIF to total the canola yields of RMs with a canola yield above 40 bu/ac.

(b) Use COUNTIF to count those RMs, then divide to get their mean.

(c) Check your answer to (b) using AVERAGEIF. The two should match.

(d) In one sentence, say when you would prefer SUMIF and COUNTIF over AVERAGEIF.

(e) A student writes =COUNTIF(canola_range,">AVERAGE(canola_range)") to count RMs above the mean, and gets 0. Explain the error in one or two sentences and give the formula that works.

Answer
    1. =SUMIF(canola_range,">40") = 5,399.60.
    1. 5,399.60 / 114 = 47.36 bu/ac.
    1. =AVERAGEIF(canola_range,">40") = 47.36 — the same.
    1. When you need the total as well as the average, or when the criterion range and the averaging range differ in a way that is easier to write out in two steps.
    1. Everything inside quotation marks is treated as literal text, so Excel searches for cells containing the characters >AVERAGE(...) and finds none. Join the operator to the computed value instead: =COUNTIF(canola_range,">"&AVERAGE(canola_range)) = 153.

Question 15

Work with the 2023 rows you extracted.

(a) Use COUNTIF twice to count the RMs whose canola yield is above 40, and the RMs whose barley yield is above 70.

(b) Use AVERAGEIF to find the mean canola yield in RMs where barley came in above 70 bu/ac. Note carefully which column is tested and which is averaged.

(c) Compare that with the overall mean canola yield and state the difference.

(d) Add an IF column flagging RMs where canola is above 40 and barley is above 70, returning Both or a blank. (IF alone is enough — nest a second IF inside the first.)

(e) Count the Both RMs with COUNTIF, and in two sentences say what the number suggests about whether good canola RMs and good barley RMs are the same places.

Answer
    1. Canola above 40: 114. Barley above 70: 96.
    1. =AVERAGEIF(barley_range,">70",canola_range) = 46.60 bu/ac — testing barley, averaging canola.
    1. The overall canola mean is 33.88, so RMs with strong barley averaged 12.72 bu/ac more canola.
    1. =IF(E2>40,IF(F2>70,"Both",""),"") — canola is column E, barley column F. The nested form is the one shown in Module 1.
    1. 86 RMs qualify on both counts. That is most of the 96 strong-barley RMs, so good canola and good barley land in largely the same places — consistent with a shared cause such as rainfall rather than anything specific to either crop.

Section 4 — PivotTables

These questions use the long version of the Saskatchewan RM data (rm_yields_1990plus_long.csv), with one row per RM-year-crop. A PivotTable can only group by a field that lives in its own column, which is why the long shape is needed here.

Question 16

(a) Build a PivotTable with Crop in Rows and average Yield in Values. Change the summary from Sum to Average.

(b) One crop shows an average in the thousands while the rest are in the tens. Name it and explain in one sentence what has gone wrong with the comparison.

(c) Copy the table from (a) and paste it as values somewhere on your sheet so it survives. Then add Year to Columns and filter to 2021–2023.

(d) Read off the average canola yield in each of those three years.

(e) In two sentences, describe what happened to canola between 2021 and 2023, and say what the Crop-only table in (a) hid.

Answer
    1. Crop in Rows, Yield in Values, set to Average.
    1. Lentils, averaging about 1,208. Lentils are reported in pounds per acre while every other crop is in bushels per acre, so the column mixes two units and the averages cannot be compared. The Unit column in the long file records which is which.
    1. Year in Columns, filtered to 2021–2023.
    1. Canola: 2021 21.86, 2022 35.40, 2023 33.88 bu/ac.
    1. Canola yields collapsed in the 2021 drought and rose about 62% the following year, holding near that level in 2023. A single all-years average of about 28 bu/ac would have hidden a swing of that size entirely.

Question 17

(a) Build a PivotTable of average Yield by Year, filtered to the Canola crop only.

(b) Identify the three worst years and the three best, with their averages.

(c) Change the summary from Average to Count. What is the count telling you, and why does it vary by year?

(d) Switch back to Average and add Count as a second Values field so both appear side by side.

(e) The counts are lower in the earliest years of the series than in recent ones. In two sentences, explain why a year built on fewer reporting RMs deserves more caution than one built on more.

Answer
    1. Year in Rows, Yield in Values set to Average, Crop filtered to Canola.
    1. Worst: 2002 (15.83), 2003 (17.03), 2001 (18.19). Best: 2025 (43.94), 2016 (42.18), 2019 (40.78).
    1. The count is the number of RMs reporting a canola yield that year. It varies because not every RM grows canola every year, and because reporting coverage changes over time.
    1. Both fields in Values: Average of Yield and Count of Yield.
    1. An average built from fewer RMs is more sensitive to any single unusual RM, so it is a less reliable summary of the province. It may also cover a different set of RMs than other years, so a year-to-year comparison is not quite like for like.

Question 18

(a) Build a PivotTable with Year in Rows and Crop in Columns, showing average Yield.

(b) Filter to the years 2019–2023 and to Canola, Spring Wheat and Barley.

(c) Read off the 2021 row. All three crops are low. What does that pattern suggest about the cause?

(d) Compute, for each of the three crops, how much its 2022 average exceeded its 2021 average.
