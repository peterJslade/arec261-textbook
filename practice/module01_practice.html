How to use this page

These ten practice questions use two synthetic datasets. Download them, open them in Excel, and answer each question by building the appropriate formula, PivotTable, or chart. Each question has a collapsible Answer section — try the question yourself before peeking.

Datasets

  • canola_yields_2025.csv — 50 canola fields in Saskatchewan in 2025, with columns field_id, region, variety, acres, yield_bu_ac, fertilizer_kg_ha, irrigated.
  • farm_costs_2025.csv — 40 input-cost orders for a grain farm in 2025, with columns order_id, order_date, supplier, product, price_per_unit, quantity, unit.

A short tip before you start: import CSVs using Data → Get Data → From Text/CSV rather than double-clicking, so Excel does not mangle any of the values.

Question 1 — Mean yield

Compute the mean yield (in bu/ac) across all 50 fields in canola_yields_2025.csv. Place the result in a labelled cell and round to two decimal places for display.

Answer
  • Expected value: 44.35 bu/ac
  • Formula: =AVERAGE(E2:E51) (assuming yield is in column E, rows 2-51)
  • Why: The mean is the sum of the values divided by the count. AVERAGE does this in one call. If your answer is off by even 0.01 bu/ac, check whether you included the header row or missed the last row.

Question 2 — Median yield

Compute the median yield (in bu/ac) for the same dataset.

Answer
  • Expected value: 45.40 bu/ac
  • Formula: =MEDIAN(E2:E51)
  • Why it matters: The median is the middle value when the data is sorted. Because it is resistant to outliers, it is often a better “typical” value than the mean, especially for skewed data. Here the median (45.4) is slightly higher than the mean (44.35), suggesting a mild left skew — a few low-yielding fields pulling the mean down.

Question 3 — Standard deviation of yield

Compute the sample standard deviation of yield across all 50 fields.

Answer
  • Expected value: 5.16 bu/ac
  • Formula: =STDEV.S(E2:E51)
  • Why STDEV.S and not STDEV.P? STDEV.S uses \(n-1\) in the denominator (Bessel’s correction) and is the right choice when your 50 fields are a sample from a larger population. STDEV.P divides by \(n\) and is only right when you literally have the entire population in front of you. For virtually all data analysis work, use STDEV.S.

Question 4 — 90th percentile of yield

Compute the 90th percentile of yield (the yield that 90% of fields fall below).

Answer
  • Expected value: 51.06 bu/ac
  • Formula: =PERCENTILE.INC(E2:E51, 0.9)
  • Interpretation: A field with a yield of 51.06 bu/ac is in the top 10% of this dataset. Percentiles are how crop insurance, government support programs, and variety trials describe performance — get comfortable with them. If you got a slightly different number with PERCENTILE.EXC, that’s fine — the two versions use different interpolation methods. For this course, use .INC.

Question 5 — Count of above-average fields

How many fields have a yield strictly greater than 48 bu/ac?

Answer
  • Expected value: 12 fields
  • Formula: =COUNTIF(E2:E51, ">48")
  • Watch out for: The criterion must be a text string in quotes when using comparison operators: ">48", not >48. This trips up almost every beginner at least once. Also note that COUNTIF with ">48" is strict — a yield of exactly 48 is not counted. If you want at-or-above, use ">=48".

Question 6 — Regional average

Compute the average yield of fields in the “South” region only.

Answer
  • Expected value: 42.41 bu/ac
  • Formula: =AVERAGEIF(B2:B51, "South", E2:E51)
  • Alternative: You could also build a PivotTable with region on Rows and yield_bu_ac as Values (set to Average). A PivotTable is more flexible if you want to see all three regional averages at once — question 7 is a good place to try that.
  • Sanity check: The South average (42.4) is lower than the overall mean (44.4), which is consistent with the hint in the dataset generator (southern fields have a slightly lower baseline yield).

Question 7 — Variety with most total acres

Which variety has the largest total acres planted across all 50 fields?

Answer
  • Expected value: DKTC 9105, with 3,090 total acres
  • How to find it: The cleanest approach is a PivotTable:
    1. Click anywhere in the data and choose Insert → PivotTable.
    2. Put variety on Rows and acres in Values (set to Sum).
    3. Sort the table by total acres descending.
  • Alternative (formula-only): =SUMIF(C2:C51, "DKTC 9105", D2:D51) will give you the total for one specific variety, but you would need to run it four times and compare. PivotTables are purpose-built for this kind of “grouped summary” question — it is worth learning them now.

Question 8 — Total production

Compute the total production (in bushels) across all 50 fields. Note that production = yield (bu/ac) × acres.

Answer
  • Expected value: 476,208.4 bushels (approximately)
  • Formula: =SUMPRODUCT(D2:D51, E2:E51)
  • Why SUMPRODUCT? It multiplies two ranges element-wise and then sums the results, all in one step. The long way would be: add a helper column F with =D2*E2 copied down, then =SUM(F2:F51). Both work, but SUMPRODUCT is cleaner and a useful tool to have in your back pocket.
  • Unit check: acres × (bu/ac) = bushels. Always stop and check that units cancel properly before trusting a computed total.

Question 9 — Spending with one supplier

In farm_costs_2025.csv, compute the total amount spent with “Prairie Ag Supply” across all orders (price per unit × quantity, summed across all their orders).

Answer
  • Expected value: $6,221.00
  • Formula: =SUMPRODUCT((C2:C41="Prairie Ag Supply")*E2:E41*F2:F41)
  • How it works: (C2:C41="Prairie Ag Supply") returns an array of TRUE/FALSE values. Multiplying converts them to 1/0, which zeros out any row that is not Prairie Ag Supply. Then SUMPRODUCT multiplies price × quantity × (1 or 0) and sums the result.
  • Alternative: Add a helper column =E2*F2 (line total), then =SUMIF(C2:C41, "Prairie Ag Supply", G2:G41). Both are valid — the helper-column version is more readable for beginners, and the SUMPRODUCT version is more compact.

Question 10 — Highest Urea price

Across all orders in farm_costs_2025.csv, what is the highest price per unit ever paid for Urea?

Answer
  • Expected value: $0.95 per kg
  • Formula: =MAXIFS(E2:E41, D2:D41, "Urea")
  • Alternative (older Excel): {=MAX(IF(D2:D41="Urea", E2:E41))} entered as an array formula with Ctrl+Shift+Enter. MAXIFS is much cleaner and works from Excel 2019 onwards.
  • Practical note: A single random draw with a fixed seed means there may only be a handful of Urea orders in this dataset. In a real analysis, always check the count of matching rows (COUNTIF(D2:D41, "Urea")) before trusting a max — a max computed from three observations is not very meaningful.
