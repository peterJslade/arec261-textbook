9  Graphing Best Practices

A chart takes a table of numbers and turns it into something a reader can take in at a glance. A good test of whether it is working is to ask whether the reader would have understood the data faster from the table itself. A good chart tells you the story immediately. A bad one makes you work through the axes, the labels and the legend before you can see what should have been obvious.

Let me illustrate with two charts. Figure 9.1 is what Tufte (2001) concludes “might well be the best statistical graphic ever drawn”. The figure, whose text is in French, documents the advance and subsequent retreat of Napoleon’s army into Russia. The width of the main line denotes the size of Napoleon’s forces, the horizontal axis represents the geography of Russia, while the bottom line graph denotes the temperature. The tan band is the army marching east and the black band is what came back: 422,000 men crossed the Polish border and 10,000 returned. You can read the disasters off it directly – at the Berezina river crossing the black band halves, and the temperature line below it reads -30° Réaumur, about -37 Celsius. I can take issue with the size of the labels in the graph, but one cannot deny the ominous story that it tells.

The second chart, Figure 9.2, was published by the Georgia Department of Public Health in May 2020. It shows confirmed COVID-19 cases in the state’s five worst-affected counties over a fifteen-day window, and the story it appears to tell is a steady decline.

However, when you read the dates along the bottom you will find that they are not-sequential – the graph is sorted by case count not by date. The story this graph imparts is wrong and it’s only when the reader strains to read every element of the graph does can some semblance of truth emerge.

The state did not defend the design. A spokesperson for the governor’s office said: “The x axis was set up that way to show descending values to more easily demonstrate peak values and counties on those dates. Our mission failed. We apologize. It is fixed.”

Hopefully, we can learn to create graphs that more are more like Figure 9.1 rather than Figure 9.2.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Say what question a chart is supposed to answer before you build it
  • Choose a chart type that suits the comparison you are making
  • Label a chart so a reader who did not make it can read it
  • Spot the common ways a chart misleads, and avoid them in your own work

9.1 The Principles

Before we get into specific chart types, some general principles. These come from a long tradition of work on data visualization, especially by Edward Tufte (The Visual Display of Quantitative Information) and more recently by Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic (Storytelling with Data).

Do you need a chart?

A chart earns its place when there is a pattern in the data that prose cannot carry. With two numbers there is no pattern, only two numbers, and a sentence does the job: in 2024 the United States produced 53.7 million tonnes of wheat and Canada produced 34.9 million. A chart of those two figures asks the reader to measure two bars to recover what the sentence already told them.

Now ask a question with a shape to it – where does Canada sit among the world’s wheat producers? – and the chart starts doing work no sentence can.

You can see at once that Canada is fifth, that the top three are in a different league, and that Canada and Australia are close enough to trade places in a bad year. Writing that out would take a paragraph, and the reader still would not see it.

Be honest

As with the case of the Georgia covid case counts above, charts can deceive as much as they can illuminate. Methods of deception include: a) sorting data . Humans minds try to recognize patterns and assign meaning – if we see dates on the horizontal axis, we expect them to be in sequential order. If we see one bars on a chart, we expect their relative size to be representative of something.

Truncating an axis is the commonest version.

Both panels show the same five malting barley varieties, which yield between 103 and 114 bushels.

Panel A has no numbers on the bars, so there is nothing to check the heights against. Starting the axis at 100 means the bars show yield minus one hundred: CDC Copeland’s 3 sits next to AAC Synergy’s 14, and a 10% gap becomes a bar four times taller. Nothing is fabricated, which is what makes it both effective and dishonest. Check where the axis starts before you read anything else.

Descriptive titles and labels

Label both axes, noting the units were applicale. In Excel, add these from Chart Design → Add Chart Element → Chart Title and → Axis Titles. Missing labels are the most common problem in student charts. Note that if you are embedding the chart in a Word document, it is generally better to put a title or caption in Word itself, rather than using the Excel built-in title.

Minimize clutter

Some charts are embellished with 3d effects, shadows, patterned fills, or tick marks. Usually these are just distracting noise. Ruthlessly remove noise that does not tell the story of the data.

Panel A has a tick every 5 bushels, gridlines in both directions, a marker on every point, a drop shadow behind every line, a shaded panel and a boxed legend. Panel B has the same four series.

Patterned fills come from an era when charts were printed in black and white and hatching was the only way to tell one bar from another. On a screen they shimmer.

A 3D bar or pie chart tilts the plane, so bars at the front read larger than identical bars at the back and you can no longer line a bar up against the axis.

Label directly

A chart where each line is labeled at the end is easier to read than one with a legend that forces the eye to jump back and forth.

Below are the same barley variety shares drawn both ways. Try finding Newdale in each.

Use text sparingly

If a reader is going to stop at something and wonder why, a few words on the plot save them the trip to the caption. The temptation is then to explain everything. Panel A annotates every bend in twenty-five years of nitrogen fertilizer prices; panel B annotates the one a reader would actually stop at.

Panel A is not wrong, and each note on its own is reasonable. Together they bury the series they are describing: the line is now the least visible thing on the plot, and a reader has no idea which of the ten notes matters. Panel B leaves the ordinary movements alone and names the one event that would otherwise send the reader looking for an explanation.

Text on a chart works the same way as everything else in this chapter. It has to earn the space it takes from the data.

More than two variables

Position on the two axes is what the eye reads best, but it is not all you have. The size of a point, its colour, its shape: each can carry another variable. Minard’s map manages six. Below is the scatter of wheat against barley yields from earlier, with the acres seeded in each zone added as the size of the point.

The extra variable answers a question the plain scatter could not: the zones at the top right are not just the highest yielding, they are also where most of the wheat is grown. Size works here because it is only being asked to rank – big, medium, small – and not to be read off precisely.

What does not work is giving a chart two different vertical scales, one on the left and one on the right. Both panels below plot canola yield and canola acres over the same five years, and the only difference between them is how the right-hand axis was scaled.

In A the lines cross in 2022 and acres appear to run ahead of yield. In B they barely meet. Nothing in the data changed. Where two series cross, which one looks higher, and whether one seems to lead the other are all decided by a choice of scale that the reader cannot see and the author may not have thought about. Plot the two series in separate panels instead, or put one of them on an index.

Use colour purposefully

Colour is not just decoration! It can be used to convey information. Colour can also be used to draw the reader’s attention to a particular finding (though one should avoid misusing this power!) or to express intensity.

Be wary of pie charts

Most books on visual display are pretty hostile to pie charts. One reason is that it it is much more difficult to compare the size of angles to the size of bars. See below the pie chart of the vote share in the 2021 Canadian election. It is kind of hard to immediately see that the Conservatives gained more votes than the Liberals in the pie chart. Much easier in the bar chart. Of course you can also put the percentages directly in the pie chart – but then why not simply use a table?

9.2 Types of Charts

Most of the work in making a good chart is picking the right kind in the first place. The choice follows from the comparison you want the reader to make, so it helps to state that comparison out loud before you open a chart menu. All the figures below are drawn from the same Saskatchewan variety data, so you can see how differently the same numbers read.

Bar charts: comparing categories

A bar chart compares a value across categories – yields by crop, acres by region, revenue by customer. The eye is good at judging the length of a bar against its neighbours, which is why bar charts do so much of the work in practice.

Two habits make bar charts better. Sort the bars by size unless the categories have their own natural order, because a sorted chart answers “which is biggest?” instantly and an alphabetical one makes the reader hunt. And start the axis at zero. A bar encodes its value by length, so cutting the axis at 30 makes a 5% difference look like a doubling. This is the single most common way a chart misleads, and it is usually the software’s default rather than anyone’s intent.

Note that the chart above runs horizontally. Most people reach for vertical bars by default, which is fine when the labels are short, but category names are often long – variety names, regions, product lines – and vertical bars leave no room for them.

In panel A the names have to be rotated to fit, so the reader tilts their head and the labels take up a third of the plot. In panel B they sit on their own line, horizontal and left aligned, and the chart can hold twenty categories as comfortably as five. Turn the chart on its side whenever the labels are longer than a word or two.

Line charts: change over time

A line chart shows how something moves over a continuous scale, almost always time. The line implies the values connect, which is exactly why you should not use one for categories: a line running from Barley to Canola to Oats suggests a progression that does not exist.

Unlike bar charts, line charts do not need a zero baseline. Nothing is encoded by length, so you are free to zoom in on the range where the action is – but say so, and do not zoom so far that ordinary variation looks like a crisis.

Scatter plots: the relationship between two variables

A scatter plot puts one variable on each axis and a point at every observation. Use it when you want to know whether two things move together.

Each point is a risk zone, with its wheat yield on one axis and its barley yield on the other. The points run up and to the right, which says a zone that grew good wheat that year generally grew good barley too – unsurprising, since both crops got the same rain. A scatter showing no pattern would be just as informative.

This is the workhorse of statistical analysis and it returns in Module 4, where we put a line through the cloud and start measuring how strong the relationship is.

Stacked bars: parts of a whole

A stacked bar breaks each bar into components, showing both the total and its composition.

The catch is that only the bottom segment sits on a common baseline. You can compare canola acres across years easily because they all start at zero; comparing the middle segments means judging the length of a floating block, which the eye does poorly.

Grouped bars

The obvious fix is to unstack the bars and set them side by side, so every bar starts at zero.

This solves the baseline problem and introduces a different one. There are now twenty bars, and the chart supports two comparisons at once: across crops within a year, and across years within a crop. The second is the one you usually want, and it is the harder of the two – following canola from 2021 to 2025 means jumping over three other bars at every step. The grouping does not help; it is in the way.

Grouped bars work when there are only two or three series and few categories, so the eye can take in a group at once. Past that they get busy quickly. When you find yourself building one, it is worth asking whether the chart is really several charts. Splitting it into a small panel per crop gives each series its own baseline and its own space:

These are called small multiples, and they scale in a way that grouped bars do not: twelve crops would be twelve panels, still readable, where twelve series in a grouped bar chart would be unusable. The trade is that comparing exact values across panels is harder, so use them when the shape of each series matters more than ranking them against each other.

Pie charts: use sparingly

A pie chart shows shares of a total. It is the most criticized chart type in the field, and the reason is worth seeing rather than taking on faith. Figure 9.19 shows the same four numbers twice.

In panel A, how much bigger is canola than spring wheat? Most people guess a much wider gap than the real one, because the eye judges angle and area poorly. In panel B you compare two bar lengths against a common baseline, and the gap is about a fifth. Neither chart is labelled with its numbers – the difference is entirely in how easily the eye reads the encoding. The pie is not useless, it does convey “canola is about half the acres” at a glance, but any comparison finer than that is easier in a bar chart.

If you use one, keep it to a handful of slices, label them directly, and never make it 3D. A 3D pie tilts the plane, which makes the near slices larger than the far ones for no reason but decoration.

Histograms and box plots

These two describe the distribution of a single variable rather than comparing categories, and you met them in Section 3.4. A histogram bins the values and shows the shape; a box plot compresses that shape into a median, a box holding the middle half of the data, and whiskers. Reach for them when the question is about spread rather than comparison.

A few more things you can do

The chart types above are the vocabulary. A handful of additions come up often enough to be worth knowing.

Shade a range behind a line. A single line hides how much variation sits behind it. Shading a band – a minimum and maximum, a middle 80%, a confidence interval – shows the average and the spread together.

Add a trend line. A straight line through a scatter summarizes the relationship the points suggest, and the shaded band around it shows how confident the fit is. Excel calls this a trendline (Chart Design → Add Chart Element → Trendline); in ggplot it is geom_smooth(). Fit one when the relationship is the point, not as decoration on every scatter you draw, and be wary of extending it past the data you have.

Draw a reference line. A horizontal line at an average, a target, a break-even price or last year’s figure turns “how big is this?” into “how does this compare?”. It is one of the cheapest ways to make a chart answer a question.

Waterfall charts show how a starting figure becomes an ending one through a series of gains and losses – gross revenue down through each cost to net margin, or last year’s profit to this year’s. Each bar floats where the previous one left off. Excel has a built-in waterfall type; ggplot has no geom_waterfall(), so panel B above is drawn with rectangles.

A few others worth naming: dot plots, which do a bar chart’s job with less ink and suit long category lists; heatmaps, for a grid of values where the pattern matters more than the numbers, like yield by zone and year; and maps, which are the natural choice for anything reported by region and which we return to in AREC 262.

Choosing

The short version:

If the question is Use
How does this value compare across categories? Bar chart
How has this changed over time? Line chart
Are these two variables related? Scatter plot
What is this total made of? Stacked bar, or a bar chart of the components
How does each of several series move? One panel per series
How is a single variable distributed? Histogram or box plot
How did we get from this figure to that one? Waterfall

When a chart does not obviously fit one of these, that is often a sign the question is not sharp enough yet.

9.3 Excel vs R for Charts

I want to be honest about this: Excel charts are fine for many purposes. They are easy to make, they look acceptable with default settings, and they are immediately editable by non-technical colleagues. If you are making a one-off chart for an internal meeting, Excel may be the right choice.

R (ggplot2) wins when you need:

  • The chart to be reproducible from a script.
  • The chart to update automatically as the underlying data changes.
  • Many similar charts (via faceting or loops).
  • Fine-grained control over appearance.
  • Statistical features (trend lines, confidence bands, quantile ribbons).

Most working analysts use both. In this course we will lean on R, because reproducibility is the harder habit to pick up later.
