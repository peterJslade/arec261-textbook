8  Merging Data

Data arrives in pieces. Suppose the question is whether canola yields in Saskatchewan track growing-season rain. The yields are in one file, from one agency. The rain is in another, from a different agency, measured at weather stations rather than in rural municipalities. Nothing in either file mentions the other. Answering the question means putting the pieces together, and that is what a merge (or join) does: it lines up rows from two tables that refer to the same thing and puts their columns side by side.

This chapter works through one merge, first in Excel and then in R. Three tables are involved, because yields and rain have no column in common and a third table is needed to connect them.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Identify the key that connects two tables, and say whether the relationship is one-to-one, many-to-one or many-to-many.
  2. Merge tables in Excel with XLOOKUP, including on a two-part key.
  3. Merge tables in R with left_join() and friends.
  4. Check what failed to match, and why a merge can return more rows than it started with.

8.1 The Three Tables

All three are real and can be downloaded:

  • rm_yields_2015_2024.csv: the Saskatchewan RM yields from Section 7.3, cut down to 2015 to 2024 and six crops. One row per RM per year, 2,950 rows, yields in bushels per acre.
  • station_precip.csv: one row per weather station per year, 160 rows, with total May to August precipitation in millimetres computed from Environment Canada’s daily records for sixteen stations across the grain belt, and a count of days with no reading.
  • rm_lookup.csv: one row per RM, 296 rows, with its name, census division and 2021 population from Statistics Canada, and the nearest of the sixteen weather stations, with the distance to it in kilometres.

The yields table knows about RMs and years. The rain table knows about stations and years. Neither knows about the other, and the lookup table is the bridge: it is the only file that says which station goes with which RM. So the merge has two steps. Attach a station to every yield row using the lookup, then attach that station’s rain for that year.

Keys

A merge matches rows on a key, a column (or columns) that both tables share and that identifies what a row is about. Before merging anything, write down what identifies a row in each table:

  • In the yields table it is RM and Year together. RM 1 appears ten times, once per year.
  • In the lookup table it is RM_Number alone. Each RM appears once.
  • In the rain table it is Station and Year together.

The first step joins yields to the lookup on RM. The second joins the result to the rain on station and year, because a station alone does not identify one rain total; it identifies ten of them.

One-to-one, many-to-one, many-to-many

How many rows on each side share a key value decides what comes back from a merge.

  • One-to-one (1:1). Each key value appears once in both tables. Two tables of provincial yields by year, joined on year. The result has the same rows as either input.
  • Many-to-one (n:1), or one-to-many. The key repeats on one side and is unique on the other. Yields to the lookup is many-to-one: ten yield rows per RM, one lookup row per RM. The result has as many rows as the “many” side, and every row picks up exactly one name and one station. Most merges are this kind.
  • Many-to-many (n:n). The key repeats on both sides. Each row on the left matches every row with the same key on the right, and the row count multiplies. Joining yields to rain on station alone would be many-to-many: each RM-year would match all ten years of its station’s rain. Occasionally that is what you want. Usually it means the key is incomplete.

Know which one you expect before you merge, and check the row count after. A many-to-one merge should return exactly as many rows as it started with.

8.2 Merging in Excel

In Excel a merge is an XLOOKUP (Section 2.2): for each row, find the matching row in the other table and bring a value across. The workbook below has the three tables on three sheets and does the merge in formula columns beside thirty of the yield rows.

The first step is the ordinary XLOOKUP. Look up the RM number in the lookup sheet and return its station:

=XLOOKUP(B5, 'RM lookup'!A:A, 'RM lookup'!E:E)

The second step needs two keys. XLOOKUP takes one, so both sides are glued together with & "|" &, the composite-key form from Section 2.2: the station and year from the yield row on one side, the station and year columns of the rain sheet on the other:

=XLOOKUP(I5 & "|" & A5,
         Precip!A:A & "|" & Precip!B:B,
         Precip!C:C)

Two things to check. An XLOOKUP that finds nothing returns #N/A, so COUNTIF(range, "#N/A") at the foot of each new column counts the rows that failed to match. And a lookup on an incomplete key does not fail. Leave the year out of the second step and XLOOKUP returns the first match for the station, so every year gets 2015’s rain, and nothing flags it. Column K of the workbook does exactly this. The check is to look at a few rows where you already know the answer.

Download this workbook

Formulas are fine at thirty rows and fine at three thousand. Past that, or when the same merge has to be redone every month, Power Query (Data > Get Data, then Merge Queries) does the join with a dialog that asks for the key columns and the join type, and records the step so it can be re-run on a new file.

The R function is left_join(). Its by argument names the key, and when the key column is called something different on each side, by = c("left_name" = "right_name") says so.

The four joins

left_join is one of a family. They differ in which rows survive.

  • left_join(a, b) keeps every row of a and fills in NA where b has no match. This is usually the one you want: your data stays intact and the extra columns come along.
  • inner_join(a, b) keeps only rows that matched in both, and drops the rest without saying so.
  • full_join(a, b) keeps everything from both, with NA wherever either side is missing.
  • anti_join(a, b) keeps rows of a with no match in b. It returns no columns from b at all, which sounds useless until you need to find out what failed to match.

First join: yields to the lookup

left_join does not complain when nothing matches. It hands you a column of NA and lets you carry on, so a join can fail completely without producing an error. anti_join is how you look, so run it first, then join, then check the row count:

yields |>
  anti_join(rms, by = c("rm" = "rm_number")) |>
  distinct(rm)

with_station <- yields |>
  left_join(rms, by = c("rm" = "rm_number"))

nrow(yields)
nrow(with_station)
> yields |>

# A tibble: 0 × 1
# ℹ 1 variable: rm <dbl>
> 
> with_station <- yields |>

> 
> nrow(yields)
[1] 2950

[1] 2950

Every RM in the yield data has a row in the lookup, and the row count is unchanged, which is what a many-to-one join should look like. You can tell dplyr what you expect and have it stop if the data disagrees:

yields |> left_join(rms, by = c("rm" = "rm_number"), relationship = "many-to-one")

If the lookup turned out to have two rows for some RM, that line errors instead of handing back extra rows.

When an anti_join does return rows, the usual reason is text. One file says "Canola/Rapeseed" and the other says "Canola"; one says "Soybean" and the other "Soybeans". Neither is wrong, but a join matches on exact text, so every such row comes back with NA. Reconciling categories is part of cleaning (Section 9.6).

Second join: add the rain

The key now has two parts. The station in with_station is called weather_station, the year is year on both sides, and both are needed:

with_station |>
  anti_join(rain, by = c("weather_station" = "station", "year")) |>
  distinct(weather_station, year)

merged <- with_station |>
  left_join(rain, by = c("weather_station" = "station", "year"))

nrow(merged)

The row-count trap

Here is the many-to-many case, arriving because year was left out of the key:

with_station |>
  left_join(rain, by = c("weather_station" = "station")) |>
  nrow()
> with_station |>
+   left_join(rain, by = c("weather_station" = "station")) |>
+   nrow()
Warning: Detected an unexpected many-to-many relationship between `x` and `y`.
ℹ Row 1 of `x` matches multiple rows in `y`.
ℹ Row 21 of `y` matches multiple rows in `x`.
ℹ If a many-to-many relationship is expected, set `relationship =
  "many-to-many"` to silence this warning.
[1] 29500

Each RM-year matched all ten years of its station’s rain, so 2,950 rows became 29,500, and every summary computed afterwards would be wrong in a way that still looks plausible. Recent versions of dplyr print a warning about an unexpected many-to-many relationship when this happens. Read it when it appears; it is telling you the key is incomplete. This is the same mistake as the station-only XLOOKUP in the Excel workbook, with the difference that Excel returns one match per row and says nothing, while R returns all of them and warns.

8.4 What the Merge Gives You

One table, one row per RM-year, with the yield and the season’s rain side by side:

merged |>
  select(year, rm, rm_name, canola, weather_station, precip_may_aug_mm)

merged |>
  filter(!is.na(canola)) |>
  group_by(year) |>
  summarise(canola_bu_ac = mean(canola),
            rain_mm = mean(precip_may_aug_mm))

The wet 2016 season has the highest canola yield in the table and the dry 2017 one of the lowest, which is the pattern you would expect. 2021 is the exception: rainfall was ordinary and yields collapsed, because that summer’s problem was heat more than drought. Rain explains some of the variation in yields and not all of it.

Two limits of this particular merge belong in the README. The nearest station is sometimes far away: RM 1, Argyle, is matched to Kipling, 145 km off, because that is the closest of sixteen stations. And a few station-years are missing three weeks of daily readings, which is what the days_missing column is for.
