Introduction: How to Think About Data Analysis

Before we touch a spreadsheet or write a line of R code, we need to spend a little time on how to think about data analysis. The specific tools change every few years — the version of Excel you learn in this class will be replaced, R may one day be replaced by something newer, and whatever machine learning library is hot right now will be a footnote in a decade. What doesn’t change are the principles: the habits of mind that separate careful analysis from careless analysis.

Why Data Analysis?

You are probably reading this because AREC 261 is required for your degree program. Fair enough. But let me try to convince you that this material matters beyond a checkbox on your transcript.

Agriculture is now one of the most data-intensive sectors of the economy. A single modern combine harvester generates gigabytes of yield, moisture, and position data in a day. A dairy operation tracks the milk output, health, and reproductive status of every cow in near-real-time. Futures markets react to satellite measurements of crop greenness in Ukraine before most farmers have finished breakfast.

Whether you end up working as a farm manager, an agronomist, a commodity trader, a crop insurer, a policy analyst, a consultant, a researcher, or something none of us can predict yet, you will be surrounded by data. The question is whether you will be good at using it or used by people who are. This course is my attempt to help you be the former.

And even if you never open a spreadsheet after this course, I hope that you can take away from this class the ability to think more clearly about data. Learning to ask “how was this measured?”, “what is being compared to what?”, and “could this pattern be a coincidence?” is a life skill. It will save you from scams, bad advice, and political nonsense.

Don’t fail to communicate!

Data analysis is not about getting the “right” number. It is about being able to correctly explain what a number means.

To correctly explain what a number means you must be able to 1) correctly calculate that number, 2) correctly interpret that number, and 3) communicate what you know. Hopefully, most of you take for granted that you should be able to calculate and interpret a number, but most students focus too much on the former and not enough on the latter. However, students (and instructors) often don’t put enough emphasis on communication.

Good communication starts with thinking about your audience — who will read this, and what do they need from it? The right form of an answer changes completely depending on who is on the other end. If you are sharing an Excel workbook, it should be easily interpretable: clear labels, a logical layout, and formatting that guides the eye to what matters. If you are sharing R code, it should be well-commented, so the next person (often future-you) can follow not just what it does but why. If you are writing a report for a manager or a client, you should lead with the finding — the “so what” — in plain language, support it with a chart or a table that makes the point at a glance, and be honest about the caveats, rather than burying the conclusion under a pile of numbers.

Note that for your module tests, 20% of your mark is based solely on your ability to communicate.

Artificial intelligence

You cannot read anything about data analysis in 2026 without encountering artificial intelligence. I want to say a few things about how AI fits into this course.

You are encouraged to use AI tools — GitHub Copilot, Claude, ChatGPT, whatever — to help you learn course content and to help you write code outside of the module tests. So, for this class, AI will mostly be a tool for your learning. However, as you progress with data analysis (both in more advanced classes and throughout your career) AI tools won’t be used to simply help you learn, but to actually undertake the analysis. So why aren’t we simply using AI in that way in this course? Why are we learning to write a script, when we could get Claude to do it for us?

At this point in the technological lifecycle, I view AI as akin to a calculator. In your University career you will never have to add, multiply, or divide large numbers by hand – you can use a calculator. Yet in elementary school we still learn these basic arithmetic operations. We learn this because we need to understand what we can ask of a calculator – that is we need to know what addition actually means. We also need to have a facility with numbers to know that when a calculator tells us that 52 × 27 is 8 million, there is some sort of error. Similarly, while AI can write code and, increasingly, help us create spreadsheets, if we don’t know what code or spreadsheets are capable of, we won’t even know what to ask AI to do, and we cannot evaluate its output properly.

How to Approach This Book

A few notes on how to get the most out of the book and the course:

  • Work through it with your hands on a keyboard. I have always found that data analysis is best learnt by doing, not by reading. While we all have our unique learning styles, I think this is true for the majority of us. The chapters are built around examples that you can follow along with and there are a large number of practice questions for you to work through.
  • Don’t skip the “why” sections. It is tempting to jump straight to the buttons and formulas. But understanding why a tool exists — what problem it solves, what it assumes — is what lets you explain your results rather than just produce them (see above). Don’t just learn how to calculate a standard deviation; learn what it means.
  • Use the external resources. If you don’t understand a concept or an Excel command, often the best thing is to have this concept or command explained to you in multiple ways. Fortunately, there are a near infinite number of resources that cover the same topics that I cover in this book. In each section, I provide a list of these resources.
  • Expect to be wrong (and frustrated) sometimes. Everyone who does data analysis makes mistakes — mislabeled columns, a decimal place in the wrong spot, a confidently wrong conclusion. The goal is not to be infallible; it is to build habits that catch mistakes quickly and make them easy to fix. Catching your own mistakes is hard: everyone who has programmed has spent hours hunting for an error, only to find an errant comma. To return to my previous discussion, AI often excels at finding our errors and explaining them to us – this is one of the best uses of AI in this course.

How the Rest of This Book Is Organized

The book is divided into three parts that mirror the modules of AREC 261:

  • Part 1 (Modules 1-7): Data Foundations. We start in Excel, because most of you have seen it before, and build up the core skills of loading, cleaning, transforming, summarizing, and visualizing data. Around Module 2 we introduce R and run the two tools in parallel for a few modules. Module 3 is about data as it actually arrives – messy, in several files, and not yet trustworthy. Module 4 covers graphing, Module 5 the AI tools you will use alongside everything else, and Module 6 how to write the result up so someone else can follow it. By Module 7 you will be comfortable describing and visualizing relationships between variables.
  • Part 2 (Modules 8-10): Probability and Simulation. We introduce the idea of a random variable and the basics of probability, then spend two modules on simulation as a way to answer probability questions without having to know any formulas. Simulation is the foundation we use for inference in the last three modules.
  • Part 3 (Modules 11-13): Inference Through Simulation. We learn about sampling variability, hypothesis testing via randomization, and bootstrap confidence intervals. The goal is to give you a solid, intuitive grasp of statistical inference before you meet the formulas in AREC 262.

Each chapter has a test bank with over 100 practice questions.
