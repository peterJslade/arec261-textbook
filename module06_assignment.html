Report and Presentation

The assignment for this module is a piece of analysis communicated two ways: a two-page report and a five-minute presentation. You will be assigned a Saskatchewan Rural Municipality, and your job is to say something true and useful about crop production there, backed by the data.

The data

Three files from the Data Appendix:

  • rm_yields_1990_2025.csv – yields for eight crops in every RM, 1990 to 2025. Lentils are in lb/ac and everything else in bu/ac; check the Unit column before comparing crops.
  • statcan_field_crops.csv – provincial seeded acres and yields, 2015 to 2025, for putting your RM in a wider context.
  • sask_variety_yields.csv – acres and yields by crop variety in each SCIC risk zone, 2021 to 2025. A risk zone is a group of RMs with similar growing conditions; find which zone your RM belongs to on SCIC’s grain risk zone map.

The RM data has yields only. There is no RM-level acreage, so any question about acreage works at the provincial or risk-zone level.

Choose one question

Answer one of the following for your RM. They differ in emphasis, not difficulty; pick the one whose answer looks most interesting once you have explored your RM’s data.

  1. The benchmark. Where do your RM’s yields stand relative to the provincial average, and has that position changed over time? A good answer compares at least two crops, shows the gap over the years rather than a single figure, and says something about why a reader should care – an RM that has gone from below average to above average is a different story from one that has always sat 10% above.

  2. The trend. How have yields in your RM changed since 1990? Which crops have improved the most, and did drought years (2021 especially) hit your RM harder or lighter than the province? A good answer separates the long-run trend from year-to-year swings and is careful about what a trend in average yields can and cannot tell you.

  3. The reliable crop. Which crop has been the most dependable in your RM? Highest average is one answer; smallest swings relative to that average is another, and they are often different crops. A good answer uses a measure of variability from Module 1 (standard deviation or the coefficient of variation), states it in words a farmer would use, and is clear that dependable and profitable are not the same thing.

  4. The provincial shift. Saskatchewan’s crop mix has shifted over the past decade – some crops have gained acres and others have lost them. Using the provincial data, which shifts are largest, and using the RM data, is your RM well placed for the crops that are growing? A good answer connects the two datasets and is honest that yields are only one part of why acres move.

  5. The new varieties. Using the variety data for the risk zone that contains your RM: how quickly are farmers adopting newer varieties of a major crop, and how does the pace compare with the province as a whole? Newer varieties usually out-yield the ones they replace – estimate the money “left on the table” by the acres still in older varieties, using the yield gap, the acres involved, and a current price you cite. A good answer treats that dollar figure with suspicion as well as interest: the variety comparison is not a controlled trial, older varieties may persist for reasons the yield column cannot see (seed cost, contracts, malting acceptance, disease packages), and the acres in each variety are suppressed in some zone-years.

Whichever question you choose, the standards are the same: numbers computed in R, charts that follow Module 4’s practices, and claims that do not go beyond what the data supports. Descriptive comparisons are what these data can carry – they cannot tell you why your RM differs from the province, and a good report does not pretend otherwise.

The two deliverables

The report is at most two pages, including figures. The chapter on report writing (18  Writing a Report) covers structure; the short version is that the report opens with the answer, supports it with one or two charts and the numbers behind them, and states its limitations. Submit the R script that produced your numbers and charts along with it – the script should run from top to bottom in a fresh session.

The presentation is five minutes, to the class, using slides. The presentations chapter (19  Presentations) covers design. Five minutes is short: expect to present one main point and one or two charts, not everything in the report. The report and the presentation answer the same question, but they are different documents – do not read the report onto slides.

You may use AI tools for this assignment under the same expectations as the rest of the course: you are responsible for every number and every claim, and you must be able to explain any code you submit.
