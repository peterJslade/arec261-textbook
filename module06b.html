17  Writing a Report

Analysis becomes useful when someone can understand the finding and use it. A report should make the answer easy to find, show enough evidence to support it and state the limits that matter for the decision.

The course project, a memo for a farm manager and a client report differ in length. Their basic structure is the same.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Structure a report around the reader’s question rather than the order of the analysis.
  2. State the main finding in the opening paragraph.
  3. Present numbers with units, comparisons and appropriate precision.
  4. Introduce a chart and tell the reader what to notice.
  5. State limitations that could change the interpretation or decision.

17.1 Lead With the Answer

A common report follows the order in which the work was done: data, cleaning, method, result and interpretation. That order records the analyst’s effort. It does not match the reader’s priorities.

Open with the finding:

TipModel opening

Black-zone wheat fields in the 2025 Saskatchewan wheat data averaged 53.0 bu/ac, compared with 38.6 bu/ac in the brown zone. The difference was 14.4 bu/ac, or 37% of the brown-zone mean. These descriptive averages exclude four fields with no reported yield and do not establish why yields differed between zones.

The paragraph states the value, the comparison and the evidence behind the interpretation. A reader with thirty seconds can stop there. The rest of the report shows how the conclusion was reached.

Write this paragraph after completing the analysis, then move it to the beginning.

17.2 A Structure That Works

A short report can use one paragraph for each item. A longer report can turn them into sections.

  1. Answer: State the main finding in plain language.
  2. Question: Explain what was asked and why the answer matters.
  3. Data: Identify the source, coverage, units, exclusions and important gaps.
  4. Method: Describe the calculation in ordinary language. Name software or functions only when they help someone reproduce the work.
  5. Results: Present the charts, tables and numbers that support the answer.
  6. Interpretation and limits: Explain what the result means and what the analysis cannot establish.
  7. Sources: Provide enough information for another person to locate the data.

The answer comes first. Qualifications follow after the evidence, where the reader can understand what they qualify.

17.3 Presenting Numbers

A calculation result is not automatically a useful reported number. 42.3851 still needs units, context and judgment about precision.

Round to the measurement

Report only the precision the data can support. Yield measured to the nearest tenth of a bushel does not justify four decimal places. Write 53.0 bu/ac, or 53 when the extra decimal would not affect the decision. Round only after the calculation is complete.

In R, round(x, 1) prepares a value for reporting. In Excel, a number format changes only the display, while =ROUND(A2, 1) returns a rounded value. Keep full precision during intermediate calculations.

Supply a comparison

“Black-zone yield averaged 53.0 bu/ac” reports a level. “Black-zone yield averaged 14.4 bu/ac, or 37%, more than the brown-zone mean” supplies both an absolute and a relative comparison. When reporting a percentage difference, name its base.

Useful comparisons include another region, an earlier year, a budget target or an established benchmark. Choose one that matches the decision.

State the units

Put units in the sentence, table header or axis label. Bushels per acre, kilograms per hectare and dollars per tonne are not interchangeable even when a column is simply named Yield or Price. In Excel, put the unit in the header, such as Yield (bu/ac), or use a custom format such as 0.0 "bu/ac"; typing the unit into each value turns numbers into text.

Report fewer numbers

Keep the figures that advance the argument. A forty-row table is not a summary when the text discusses only three values. Move supporting detail to an appendix or provide the data file separately. In a handoff workbook, the report should draw from a compact Output sheet while the Raw and Cleaned sheets preserve the detailed record (Section 16.2).

17.4 Tables and Charts

Use a table when the reader needs exact values. Use a chart when the comparison, pattern or change matters more than individual numbers.

Table Table 17.1 reports the exact values behind the opening paragraph.

Introduce each table or chart before it appears. Name what it displays, then state the feature that supports the argument:

Figure Figure 17.1 compares mean 2025 wheat yield across the three soil zones. The black-zone mean is highest, and the brown-zone mean is lowest.

This tells the reader where to look and why the figure is present. A chart without an introduction leaves the reader to decide what matters.

Aim for one chart, one point. If the main message needs two unrelated sentences, the chart may be doing two jobs.

Captions should identify the measure, groups, time period and units. They should not repeat the whole paragraph above the figure.

17.5 Describe the Method, Not the Keystrokes

The method section should let a knowledgeable reader understand and reproduce the analysis without becoming a diary of software commands.

Write:

TipWrite

We calculated the mean 2025 wheat yield for each soil zone. Four of the 150 fields had no reported yield and were excluded from the averages.

WarningDo not write

We clicked Data, made a PivotTable, dragged yield_bu_ac to Values and changed the calculation to Average.

Do not list each menu choice or function call unless the implementation itself matters. Keep the script or workbook as the detailed record. A usable workbook record leaves formulas in place, labels the summary cells that feed the report and documents sources or Power Query steps on a README sheet.

For a fixed Word or PDF report, format a small summary range in Excel and paste it using Keep Source Formatting or Paste as Picture. A picture will not update when the workbook changes. For charts, see the export choices in Section 11.5. Do not retype calculated values into the report.

17.6 Saying What You Do Not Know

A useful limitation is one that could change the reader’s interpretation or action.

  • Coverage: insured acres only, one province or a short time period.
  • Missing data: observations or variables absent in a systematic way.
  • Assumptions: a weighting rule, category mapping or price used in a calculation.
  • Confounding: differences between groups that the available data cannot separate.
  • Causation: an observed relationship that was not created by a controlled experiment.

State each limitation plainly and explain its consequence. “The data include insured acres only, so the provincial average may not represent uninsured production” is more useful than “results should be interpreted with caution.”

Do not bury the answer beneath qualifications, and do not omit a limitation that could reverse the decision.

17.7 The Course Project

The course project asks a question about agricultural data, carries out a reproducible analysis and explains the finding in a short report. Use the structure in this chapter. Submit the report together with the script or workbook that produced its numbers and figures, following the detailed submission instructions provided for the project.

Complexity is not the goal. A direct comparison with a clear chart, traceable calculations and honest limits is stronger than an elaborate method the reader cannot follow.
