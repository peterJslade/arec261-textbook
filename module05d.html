16  AI Tools in Excel

The last two chapters used AI from inside Positron, writing R. The same help now exists inside Excel. All three of the major AI companies ship a version of their assistant that sits in a panel beside your spreadsheet, reads the workbook you have open, and – increasingly – edits it for you.

This chapter is short, because the skills are the ones you already have. The prompting habits from Section 13.4 and the checking habits from Section 13.5 carry over directly; only the buttons are new. The landscape also changes monthly, so treat the product details here as a snapshot from mid-2026.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Name the three main AI assistants available inside Excel and what access each requires.
  2. Explain what “agentic” means: the assistant plans and executes multi-step work in the workbook, rather than suggesting steps for you to take.
  3. Give an agent an outcome to produce, and then verify what it actually did.
  4. Decide when agentic Excel work is appropriate in this course.

16.1 The Three Assistants

Assistant Where it lives What you need
Microsoft Copilot Built into Excel’s ribbon A Microsoft 365 Copilot licence, sold as an add-on to Microsoft 365
Claude for Excel An add-in from the Office marketplace; opens as a sidebar A paid Claude plan (Pro, Max, Team or Enterprise)
ChatGPT for Excel An add-in from the Office marketplace; opens as a side panel An OpenAI account; available on all plans, including the free tier

All three do the same family of things. They read the sheets you have open, so you can ask questions about your own data (“which varieties lost acreage between 2021 and 2025?”). They explain what is already there: what a formula does, where a cell’s value comes from, why a lookup returns #N/A. And they write into the workbook: a formula column, a summary table, a chart, a PivotTable.

The differences that matter to a student are access and reach. Copilot is the most deeply integrated but needs a licence your personal Microsoft account probably does not have. ChatGPT’s add-in is the one everyone can try, because it works on a free account. Claude’s requires a paid plan. Check what your university account currently includes before assuming any of them is available on the lab machines.

16.2 What “Agentic” Means in a Spreadsheet

The first generation of these tools was conversational: you asked, it suggested a formula, you pasted the formula in. The current generation is agentic: you describe an outcome, and the assistant plans the steps, executes them in the workbook, inspects its own results, and revises until it is satisfied. Microsoft calls this Agent Mode; Claude and ChatGPT’s add-ins work the same way without the branding.

Concretely, an agent given the barley workbook from Chapter 11 and the instruction “make a bar chart of the varieties with over 10,000 acres, sorted, with labelled axes” will filter, sort, build the chart and format it – the whole sequence Module 4 taught, done in one instruction. Given the messy grain-delivery file from Chapter 8, “clean this up: fix the crop names, flag the impossible weights, and give me a summary by crop” produces a multi-step cleaning pass. The agent is doing real work, in your file, across many cells, without asking about each step.

An assistant that suggests a formula cannot damage anything you did not paste. An agent that edits can overwrite formulas with values, delete a helper column it decides is clutter, or fill a column using a rule you would not have chosen – and a workbook does not keep a record the way a script does. Every result the agent produces was produced by code you never saw.

16.3 Verifying Agentic Work

The checking discipline is the same as Section 13.5, with two Excel-specific habits added:

  • Work on a copy. Before letting an agent loose on a workbook, duplicate the file, or at minimum the sheet. The raw-data-stays-raw rule (Section 7.6) exists precisely for this moment.
  • Read the change summary. All three assistants report what they did – cells changed, columns added, steps taken. Read it the way you would read a co-worker’s description of what they did to your file, then spot-check: row counts before and after, one or two values recomputed by hand, totals against the source.
  • Ask for formulas, not values. An agent pasting the result of a calculation leaves you a dead number; the same calculation as a formula leaves something you can inspect and rerun. If the summary says values were pasted, ask it to redo the step with formulas.
  • Make it explain. “Walk me through what you did to column D” is a legitimate follow-up, and the explanation is checkable against the cells.

16.4 In This Course

The course policy (Section 13.10) applies unchanged: you may use these tools for practice and for the project, you remain responsible for every number you submit, and the tests are done without them. For the Excel tests in particular, the skills the tests examine – building the chart, writing the lookup, constructing the PivotTable – are exactly the ones the agent would otherwise do for you, which is why they are tested without it.

An agent speeds up work you already know how to check. If you cannot check the work, the speed is not helping you.
